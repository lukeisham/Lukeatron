# GMAT Focus Edition — Critical Reasoning — US

**Reference test:** GMAT Focus Edition — Critical Reasoning (US business school admissions)
**Format:** Critical Reasoning questions embedded in the Verbal Reasoning section. Each item presents a short argument (~100 words) followed by one multiple-choice question. Approximately 10–13 CR questions per exam.
**Answer style:** Single correct answer (A–E). Binary scoring.
**Question types:** Weaken, strengthen, assumption, inference, evaluate the argument, explain the paradox, boldface (identify the roles of bolded statements), complete the argument.

**Distinctive vs. LSAT:** GMAT CR passages are often business, economics, or policy-oriented. Questions tend to have a more practical, real-world flavour than LSAT LR's more abstract logical analysis.

## GMAT CR — Evaluate the Argument Question

**Difficulty:** Medium

### Stimulus

Premier Foods, a national supermarket chain, plans to increase profitability by replacing its store-brand budget product line with a new premium store-brand line. The company's analysis shows that premium store-brand products carry a 45% profit margin, compared to only 18% for the budget line. Furthermore, consumer surveys indicate that 64% of shoppers would be willing to pay more for higher-quality store-brand items. Therefore, the switch to premium products will significantly increase Premier Foods' overall profitability.

### Question

Which of the following would be most useful to know in order to evaluate the argument?

### Options

- **A:** Whether Premier Foods' competitors also offer premium store-brand product lines.
- **B:** Whether the volume of premium products sold would be comparable to the volume of budget products sold.
- **C:** Whether the consumer survey was conducted by an independent research firm or by Premier Foods' internal marketing team.
- **D:** Whether the cost of developing and marketing the new premium line would exceed the additional revenue generated.
- **E:** Whether Premier Foods' customers are primarily motivated by price or by quality when choosing where to shop.

### Correct Answer

**B**

### Clue

The argument relies on a critical unstated comparison: profit margin is a *per-unit* metric, but total profitability depends on both margin *and* volume. What critical piece of information is missing?

### Explainer

**B is correct.** The argument commits the classic error of assuming that a higher profit margin automatically translates to higher total profit. Total profit = (profit per unit) × (number of units sold). If Premier Foods sells far fewer units of the premium line (because higher prices reduce demand, or because shelf space constraints limit volume), total profit could actually decrease despite the higher margin. Knowing whether volumes would be comparable is essential to evaluating the argument's conclusion.

- **A** is about competitive positioning, not about whether the strategy would increase Premier Foods' own profitability.
- **C** is about the survey's credibility, but even if the survey is perfectly accurate about willingness to pay, it doesn't answer the volume question.
- **D** is a cost-benefit question, but the argument's flaw is about the revenue side (margin × volume), not about development costs. Even if development costs are low, the strategy fails if volume collapses.
- **E** is about customer motivation generally, but the survey already addresses willingness to pay. The missing piece is not whether customers *would* pay more, but how *many* would do so at the premium price point.
