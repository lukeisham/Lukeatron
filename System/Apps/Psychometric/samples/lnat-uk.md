## LNAT Passage — The Right to Be Forgotten

**Difficulty:** Hard

### Stimulus

In 2014, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that individuals have the right to request that search engines remove links to information about them that is "inadequate, irrelevant, or no longer relevant." This ruling, often described as establishing a "right to be forgotten," has generated fierce debate about the balance between privacy and freedom of expression. Supporters argue that in an age when the internet preserves every indiscretion indefinitely, individuals deserve a mechanism to escape their past. A youthful conviction for a minor offence, a bitter divorce, an embarrassing photograph — these should not, in perpetuity, define a person's digital identity. The argument is not merely about compassion; it is about proportionality. The law has long recognised that after a certain period, spent convictions should not prejudice employment prospects or social standing. The right to be forgotten, on this view, simply extends this principle to the digital sphere.

Opponents raise two principal objections. The first is practical: the ruling places private companies — search engine operators — in the position of arbiters of public interest, deciding what the public may and may not know without any democratic mandate to do so. Google, for instance, has received over a million delisting requests since 2014, and each requires a judgement call about whether the information serves a public interest. Critics charge that this is effectively privatised censorship. The second objection is principled: the very concept of a right to be forgotten is incompatible with open societies. If information is lawfully published, citizens should be able to access it. Rewriting the historical record, even in the limited sense of making it harder to find, is a form of collective self-deception. The public has a legitimate interest in knowing, for example, whether a politician or public figure has a history of dishonesty, regardless of how long ago the relevant events occurred.

A middle-ground position holds that the right to be forgotten is defensible in principle but that its implementation should be judicial rather than administrative — that is, delisting decisions should be made by courts applying established legal criteria, not by technology companies making ad hoc assessments. This would preserve the right while addressing the "privatised censorship" objection. However, this approach raises its own difficulties. Courts are slow, legal proceedings are expensive, and the sheer volume of delisting requests would overwhelm any judicial system. The middle ground may be principled in theory but unworkable in practice.

### Questions

**1.** Which of the following best captures the main argument of the passage?

- **A:** The right to be forgotten should be abolished because it constitutes privatised censorship.
- **B:** The right to be forgotten extends existing legal principles of proportionality to the digital sphere, but its implementation raises serious practical and principled concerns.
- **C:** Courts, rather than technology companies, should be responsible for making delisting decisions.
- **D:** The public has a legitimate interest in accessing all lawfully published information about public figures.
- **E:** Google has received too many delisting requests to make principled decisions about each one.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

**Explanation:** B captures the passage's balanced presentation of both the argument in favour (extension of proportionality principles) and the two main objections (privatised censorship, incompatibility with open societies). A, C, and D each represent one side, not the overall argument. E is a factual detail, not the main argument.

**2.** The author mentions "spent convictions" in paragraph 1 primarily in order to:

- **A:** Argue that criminal records should never be publicly accessible.
- **B:** Provide an analogy drawn from existing legal practice to support the proportionality argument for the right to be forgotten.
- **C:** Suggest that the right to be forgotten is primarily important for people with criminal records.
- **D:** Distinguish between criminal and civil matters in privacy law.
- **E:** Demonstrate that the law already recognises limits on the use of past information.

**Correct Answer:** **E**

**Explanation:** The spent convictions example is used to illustrate that "the law has long recognised" limits on how past information can be used — i.e., the principle already exists. This is an analogy supporting the extension to the digital sphere. B says "analogy" which is close but E better captures the author's purpose of showing existing legal recognition. B implies the author is making the proportionality argument themselves; E correctly identifies that the author is pointing to existing law as evidence.

**3.** Which of the following is an unstated assumption in the opponents' argument that the right to be forgotten is "incompatible with open societies"?

- **A:** Open societies require that all lawfully published information remains equally accessible to all citizens.
- **B:** Search engines are the primary means by which citizens access information.
- **C:** Politicians and public figures should be treated differently from private individuals.
- **D:** The right to be forgotten is the only mechanism by which information can be made harder to find.
- **E:** European data protection law is incompatible with democratic governance.

**Correct Answer:** **A**

**Explanation:** The opponents' claim that restricted access to lawfully published information is incompatible with open societies assumes that open societies require unrestricted access to all such information. If one accepts that some restrictions on information access are compatible with open societies (e.g., libel law, court injunctions, classified information), the opponents' principled objection weakens. A identifies this assumption.

**4.** The passage describes the middle-ground proposal as "principled in theory but unworkable in practice." Which of the following, if true, would MOST strengthen the case that the middle ground is viable?

- **A:** The majority of delisting requests are straightforward and could be resolved through an automated triage system.
- **B:** Google has improved the accuracy of its delisting decisions since 2014.
- **C:** Public support for the right to be forgotten has increased across Europe.
- **D:** Several countries have successfully integrated delisting decisions into existing small-claims court procedures without significant delays.
- **E:** The European Commission has proposed new legislation to clarify delisting criteria.

**Correct Answer:** **D**

**Explanation:** The objection to the middle ground is specifically that courts are "slow" and "expensive" and that the volume of requests would "overwhelm" the judicial system. D directly rebuts this objection by providing evidence that judicial handling is feasible. A addresses a different proposal (automated triage). B addresses Google's decisions, not courts. C is about public opinion, not feasibility. E is about clarifying criteria, not about whether courts can handle the volume.
