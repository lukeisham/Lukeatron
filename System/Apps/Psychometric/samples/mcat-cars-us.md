## MCAT CARS Passage — The Ethics of Triage

**Difficulty:** Hard

### Stimulus

The concept of triage — the systematic prioritisation of patients based on the severity of their condition and the likelihood of benefitting from treatment — emerged from military medicine in the early nineteenth century. Dominique Jean Larrey, Napoleon's chief surgeon, developed the first formal triage system during the Napoleonic Wars, establishing the principle that the wounded should be treated not according to rank or nationality but according to medical need. This was, for its time, a radically egalitarian departure from the hierarchical norms of European society. Larrey's innovation was not merely technical; it was deeply philosophical. By asserting that a wounded enemy soldier merited treatment before a wounded general if the enemy's wounds were more severe, Larrey subordinated social status to clinical urgency.

Contemporary bioethics has refined Larrey's egalitarian impulse into a set of competing principles. The utilitarian approach — maximise the greatest good for the greatest number — often translates in practice to treating those most likely to survive first, even if this means withholding care from the most critically ill. This is the logic behind disaster triage protocols such as START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment), which categorises patients into colour-coded groups: those who will survive without immediate treatment (green), those who require immediate treatment to survive (red), those who will survive only with treatment that cannot be provided under current constraints (black), and those with non-life-threatening injuries (yellow). Under START, the red category receives priority, even if this means a black-tagged patient — someone who might theoretically benefit from heroic intervention — receives no care at all.

Critics of utilitarian triage argue that it smuggles a consequentialist logic into medical ethics that is fundamentally at odds with the physician's fiduciary duty to the individual patient. The physician, on this view, ought to treat each patient as an end in themselves rather than as a variable in a population-level optimisation problem. The utilitarian calculus, critics contend, asks physicians to abandon the very ethos that distinguishes medicine from public administration: the unconditional commitment to the person in front of them. Proponents respond that in conditions of absolute scarcity — a battlefield, a pandemic, a natural disaster — refusing to triage is itself an ethical decision, one that passively allows the maximum number of preventable deaths to occur through inefficiency rather than through explicit choice. On this view, refusing to triage is simply triage by default, where the implicit principle is first-come-first-served, which is no less an ethical framework than START — merely a less transparent one.

The tension between these positions is not easily resolved, because it reflects a deeper philosophical disagreement about the nature of moral responsibility. If one believes that morality is primarily about the quality of one's actions — treating each patient with dignity, refusing to instrumentalise human beings — then utilitarian triage appears morally impermissible regardless of its outcomes. If one believes that morality is primarily about the state of the world one brings about — minimising suffering, maximising lives saved — then refusing to triage appears as a form of moral squeamishness that prioritises the physician's clean conscience over patients' lives. Neither position is easily dismissed, which is why the ethics of triage remains one of the most contested domains in bioethics, not despite its apparent simplicity but precisely because it forces us to articulate what we believe medicine is ultimately for.

### Questions

**1.** Which of the following best expresses the main idea of the passage?

- **A:** Triage protocols such as START represent the most ethically defensible approach to resource-limited medicine.
- **B:** The tension between utilitarian and deontological approaches to triage reflects a fundamental philosophical disagreement about the nature of moral responsibility.
- **C:** Dominique Jean Larrey's original egalitarian triage system has been largely abandoned in contemporary bioethics.
- **D:** Physicians should resist population-level thinking because it conflicts with their fiduciary duty to individual patients.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

**Explanation:** B captures the passage's central argument: the debate over triage is ultimately about whether morality concerns actions or outcomes. A is advocacy, not analysis. C is factually incorrect (the passage says Larrey's ideas were refined, not abandoned). D is one side of the debate, not the main idea.

**2.** The author most likely mentions "first-come-first-served" in paragraph 3 in order to:

- **A:** Advocate for a specific alternative to START triage.
- **B:** Illustrate that efficiency-based triage is superior to other allocation methods.
- **C:** Suggest that all resource allocation methods involve implicit ethical frameworks.
- **D:** Criticise emergency departments that do not use formal triage systems.

**Correct Answer:** **C**

**Explanation:** The author argues that "refusing to triage is simply triage by default" — the point is that every allocation method embeds an ethical principle, whether acknowledged or not. C captures this argument. A, B, and D misrepresent the author's neutral-analytic stance.

**3.** Based on the passage, a critic of utilitarian triage would be most likely to agree with which of the following statements?

- **A:** The greatest good for the greatest number should always guide resource allocation decisions.
- **B:** A physician's primary obligation is to the individual patient, not to aggregate welfare.
- **C:** Larrey's original triage system was ethically superior to contemporary protocols.
- **D:** Consequentialist reasoning has no legitimate role in medical ethics.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly states that critics believe "the physician ought to treat each patient as an end in themselves." B restates this. A is the utilitarian position critics oppose. C and D are not stated — critics object to utilitarian triage specifically, not to all of Larrey's system or all consequentialism.

**4.** Suppose a new triage protocol were developed that assigned treatment priority based on a patient's estimated future social contribution (e.g., years of productive work remaining). How would this protocol MOST relate to the debate described in the passage?

- **A:** It would be consistent with both the utilitarian and deontological frameworks.
- **B:** It would represent a third framework not reducible to either utilitarianism or deontology.
- **C:** It would exacerbate the critics' concern that triage instrumentalises patients by treating them as variables in a calculus.
- **D:** It would resolve the tension described in the passage by providing an objective metric for prioritisation.

**Correct Answer:** **C**

**Explanation:** This is a classic CARS "application" question. Social-contribution triage is an extreme form of the consequentialist logic that critics already object to — it further treats patients as means to an end (societal productivity). C correctly identifies that this would intensify, not resolve, the critics' concerns.
