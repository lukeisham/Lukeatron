## Watson-Glaser — Evaluation of Arguments

**Difficulty:** Medium
**Section:** Evaluation of Arguments

### Stimulus

**Statement:** "Should the government require all new residential buildings to include solar panels?"

### Instructions

Below are four arguments in favour of or against the statement. For each argument, determine whether it is **Strong** (both important and directly relevant to the question) or **Weak** (either unimportant or not directly relevant to the question). An argument is strong only if it directly addresses the statement and provides a significant reason for or against.

### Arguments

**A:** Yes — solar panels reduce household electricity bills by an average of £200–£400 per year, which would help low-income families manage energy costs.

**B:** No — the initial cost of solar panel installation would increase house prices, potentially making homeownership less accessible for first-time buyers.

**C:** No — solar panel manufacturing produces carbon emissions, so the environmental benefit may not be as large as supporters claim.

**D:** Yes — several European countries including Germany and Spain already have similar requirements, and their energy sectors have not experienced significant disruption.

### Correct Answer Key

| Argument | Strength | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Strong | Directly relevant — provides a concrete, verifiable benefit of the proposed policy. The connection between solar panels and household savings is a legitimate pro-argument. |
| **B** | Strong | Directly relevant — identifies a genuine cost-side concern. Higher upfront construction costs and their impact on affordability is a substantive counter-argument. |
| **C** | Weak | Although it sounds sophisticated, this is a classic "perfect solution" fallacy. The argument concedes there is a benefit but dismisses it because it isn't total. All manufacturing has some carbon footprint; the relevant question is net environmental gain over the panel's lifetime, which this argument ignores. |
| **D** | Weak | This is an appeal to common practice. The fact that other countries do something is not, by itself, a strong reason for doing it. Their circumstances may differ, and the argument does not explain *why* their experience is transferable. |

### Clue

A strong argument must be both **important** and **directly related** to the statement. Watch for arguments that sound plausible but rely on logical fallacies (appeal to common practice, perfect solution fallacy, straw man, etc.).

### Explainer

Watson-Glaser Evaluation of Arguments tests the ability to distinguish between arguments that are genuinely relevant and significant vs. those that are superficially appealing but logically weak. Key traps include: (1) arguments that appeal to authority or common practice without establishing relevance; (2) arguments that dismiss a proposal because it doesn't solve every aspect of a problem; (3) arguments that are emotionally compelling but factually irrelevant. The correct approach is to ask: "If this were true, would it provide a good reason to accept or reject the proposal?"
