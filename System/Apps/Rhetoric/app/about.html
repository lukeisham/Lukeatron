Rhetoric is an offline reference to rhetorical devices and fallacies, kept for teaching preparation. This page documents how each score, label and text field is produced.

Popularity

A 0–100 score for how often a device’s name appears in printed English books (Google Books Ngram, 1980–2019). Higher means more frequent.

Method

  1. Query the device’s name and each alias separately, case-insensitive. Each query returns the yearly share of all printed words; the shares are averaged without smoothing. The device keeps its largest count. Counts are never summed, so no book is counted twice.
  2. Names with a commoner everyday sense (“climax”, “irony”, “apostrophe”, “red herring”) are also queried inside device phrases: “use of …” and “rhetorical …” for devices; “… fallacy”, “… argument” and “fallacy of …” for fallacies. The phrase count is multiplied by about 95 (the typical bare-name-to-phrase ratio for unambiguous devices) and capped at the bare-name count. If the phrases fall below Ngram’s threshold, the device gets about one seventh of its bare-name count. Names whose everyday meaning is the device itself (metaphor, hyperbole, euphemism) are used unchanged.
  3. Aliases that are ordinary words (“reference”, “saying”, “contrast”) or belong to another field (“meiosis”, “parison”, “communicatio”) are not queried. The main name always is.
  4. Terms used mainly in formal logic, philosophy or statistics are multiplied by 0.25: Modus Ponens, Modus Tollens, Constructive Dilemma, Reductio ad Absurdum, Syllogism, Inference to the Best Explanation, Principle of Charity, Argument from Expert Opinion, Naturalistic Fallacy, Is-Ought Fallacy and Ecological Fallacy.
  5. Ngram queries are limited to five tokens and count a hyphen as a token. A hyphenated name is queried whole, and also as the part after its last hyphen when that part is two words or more (“Is-Ought Fallacy” also as “ought fallacy”). Names too long to query are skipped. Brackets and commas are stripped first.

Score

With m the final count in mentions per billion words and M the top device’s count (Metaphor, about 6,300):

score = round(100 × ln(1 + m) ÷ ln(1 + M))

The log scale keeps the few very frequent devices from compressing everything else toward zero.

Limits

  • Ngram omits phrases found in fewer than 40 books, so many classical names score 0. That means rare in print, not unimportant.
  • Below about 0.5 mentions per billion words, differences are noise: those devices score 0–5 and their relative order means little.
  • A Flipside is a name coined for this app, so no book uses it; it inherits its fallacy’s score.
  • Only counts are stored; no book text is copied.

Source

  • Google Books Ngram Viewer: year-by-year frequency of words and phrases in Google’s scanned English books.

AI confidence labels

A stored label (high, medium or low): the AI’s confidence that a device’s description, example and placement are jointly correct. It is set when the data is built and is never recomputed in the browser.

Tests

TestPasses when…
1. Description The device has one settled meaning, and either two or more of the reference sources under “Sources” name it, or a published work outside that list gives the same sense. Commercial and hobby sites do not count. A description resting on Wikipedia alone fails, even if listed sources name the device.
2. Example An example exists that identifies the device without its name. A fallacy’s example must visibly commit the error.
3. Placement Its Form, Function and Category placements follow the written criteria directly, with no judgement call between close headings.

Labels

PassesLabelReading
3HighSafe to teach from as it stands

0 or 1LowDraft; check before use

  • A description sourced only from Wikipedia fails Test 1, so its label drops: Medium at most.
  • A Flipside is rated on its own and caps at Medium: its name appears in no source.
  • Tests 2 and 3 are the AI’s own judgement. The label says where to look closely; it does not replace reading.

Descriptions

Every description is written by the AI, not copied, and written against the sources: it checks them when writing a description, wherever a source exists. The display options call it the definition.

Form

One or two sentences: what the device is and what it does to the reader or listener. It states the defining move, not an example. A Flipside describes the same move as its fallacy, then what makes this use fair, as an instruction to the speaker (“Cite a genuine expert…”).

Verification

Sources are tried in this order, stopping at the first that defines the device:

  1. The confidence sources listed below (the ones behind Test 1 in §2), plus published works that name the device in the same sense (Rhetorica ad Herennium, Quintilian, Peacham). Commercial and hobby sites do not count.
  2. Wikipedia, only if none of those defines it. A Wikipedia-only description fails Test 1, because Wikipedia never counts as a source there, so the device’s confidence label is lowered (Medium at most).
  3. Luke, if neither defines it. The AI says which sources it tried and asks; it does not write a description unaided.

Whichever source is used, the AI reads the entry or passage itself. A search summary, AI summary or memory is not a check. The two definitions are then compared and given one result:

ResultMeaning
AgreesSame sense; nothing claimed beyond the source.
NarrowerOmits part of the source’s sense.
BroaderClaims more than the source.
Different senseThe source means something else by the same name.

Only “agrees” counts as checked. Any other result is fixed with the smallest edit that restores agreement; an agreeing description is left alone. After an edit, the device’s examples and placements are rechecked and its label is re-scored.

Disputed or unsourced

  • Rival senses. The main sense comes first, the other in one closing sentence. If the sources cannot be reconciled, the device fails Test 1.
  • Flipsides. The coined name appears in no source, so the description is checked against its fallacy’s verified one: remove only the fault, and what remains must still be the same move.
  • Other unsourced devices. Luke is asked (see above); until he answers, the description counts as unchecked. The app never cites a source that was not read.

What “checked” means

“Agrees” means it matches the sources read; where sources themselves disagree, the question is not settled. The app stores no per-description flag. The AI confidence label is the visible signal.

AI examples

Every device has exactly one example that the AI wrote itself, shown beside its real quote (§5) and never replaced by it. It illustrates the description in a single plain sentence.

Requirements

  • Complete and exclusive. Every part of the description is visible, and no other device is. Someone who did not know the name could identify it.
  • Marked. The part the device depends on (letter, syllable, word, ending, word order, punctuation) is set in italics, and only that part. A device that depends on no one part has none.
  • Fallacies and Flipsides. A fallacy’s example commits the error as an argument. A Flipside’s makes the same move without the fault, with its evidence in view.
  • Self-contained. Shortest wording that works; at most one short bracketed note, which never names the device.
  • Not a quotation. It is never credited to anyone: no author, work or date, so it cannot pass as a real quote.
  • Clean and English. No swear words or sexual content. A device that depends on another language gets no AI example, only a real quote with a translation.

Check

Each part of the description is ticked off against the example. Italicised letters and endings are counted; repeated words are read aloud in the sentence. A missing or extra part means a rewrite, and a changed description means rechecking its example.

Real quotes

Where one exists, a device carries one real quote: words a named person, or a long-standing common saying, actually used. It shows the device in use. A device without one shows only its AI example. No quote is ever invented.

Selection

  • Clean match. Every part of the description is present, the device is what the quote is built on, and the device can be identified from the quote alone.
  • Best instance. The handbook-standard instance beats a more famous but looser one. Take the shortest passage that still works, joining separate parts with an ellipsis.
  • Behaviour. A fallacy’s quote commits the error as an argument. A Flipside’s shows the fair use with its evidence visible.
  • Clean language. A quote with swear words or sexual content is not used and is not edited, since an altered quote is no longer a real one. Other offensive material, such as hostile views or propaganda, is allowed where it is the best verified match.

Verification

The wording, speaker and locator (chapter, scene or date) are checked against the work itself: full text, official transcript, or a reputable edition or scan. A search summary, quotation site or memory is not a check. A song is checked against its official lyrics. A common saying with no single author must appear in at least two independent collections, with its earliest form given; if an author turns up, that person is credited.

How a quote is credited and dated

The author is named. A fictional character’s words in a book, play or poem are credited to the writer, with the character named in the quote; a film character’s words are credited to the character; a song to its songwriter; a saying with no known author is marked unattributed, with its earliest source. The work and passage go in a closing bracket, titles in italics. “Attributed to” is never used.

When it can be found quickly, the date the words were first said or published is recorded too (a full date for a speech or dated article, a year for a book or poem; AD 60 or 935 BC for the ancient world); the Index sorts by it. It is never guessed or searched for at length, with one exception: the books of the Bible, Shakespeare’s plays and a few other ancient or medieval works are dated by the year scholars (or, for the others, Wikipedia) most often give for when they were written, and that year is an estimate. An unknown date is left blank and the quote is listed as “Date unknown”. The date is the original’s, not a translation’s or edition’s.

Translations

A foreign-language quote carries an English translation by a named translator whose wording has been read. If none is usable and the device really exists in that language, the AI may translate, and the closing note says so. Modernised spelling is noted.

Loose fits

A quote that falls short on any point above is a stand-in. It is kept only after a search across several works and kinds of source, and is replaced when a cleaner one turns up.

Methodology

Two published articles, one from writing pedagogy and one from computational linguistics, shape the model: a device is understood by what it does and where it is used, not only by its name.

Fallacies depend on the audience

Womack (2015) surveyed thirteen rhetoric textbooks and found most treat a fallacy as a context-free logic error, which trains students to hunt for errors and reject any uncertain claim. She treats it instead as a rhetorical failure: the same move persuades one audience and fails with another, so naming a fallacy is itself an argument that needs reasons. Her method sets each fallacy beside the sound strategy it resembles (false analogy beside strong analogy) and has students identify the audience’s knowledge and beliefs first. In this app that is the Flipside: each fallacy paired with its fair counterpart.

Figures pair a pattern with a purpose

Harris, Di Marco, Ruan and O’Reilly (2018) define a figure as a surface pattern in the wording, such as a phrase repeated in reverse order, that reliably carries a kind of meaning. Because the pattern is visible in the text, software can detect it and infer the meaning, which supports mining arguments from large corpora. They note that figures overlap within one sentence and that traditional names are inconsistent (one label for several patterns, several labels for one). Their remedy is an XML annotation scheme that tags the defining parts of each figure, preserving overlaps. In this app that is the split between Form (the pattern) and Function (the purpose).

Consequences for the app

  • Three independent lists (Category, Form, Function), not one final taxonomy. Each device sits once in Form, once in Function, and in one or more Categories.
  • An AI confidence label on every device, so weak descriptions and hard placements stay visible.
  • An AI example (the description shown exactly) paired with a real quote (the device in use).

These are design choices for this app. The articles support them but do not prescribe them.
