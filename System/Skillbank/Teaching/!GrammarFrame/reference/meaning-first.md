# meaning-first.md — the governing frame

The standing doctrine for `!GrammarFrame`. Every heading in both rendered guides is written
through this frame. The four research sources supply **what** English does; this file supplies
**why** it does it. Where the two disagree about the why, this file wins and the source becomes
plain data.

This is a living file. When a run settles a new principle, append it here rather than leaving it
in one topic's prose — the frame is meant to accumulate.

---

## The one claim underneath everything

**Grammar is the shape meaning takes when it has to come out one word at a time.**

A speaker means something whole and all at once. Speech is a line — one word, then the next. Every
grammatical rule is a consequence of that translation: something whole, pushed through something
linear. So no rule is arbitrary. Each one is a decision about what to put first, what to put next,
and what to mark, in order to rebuild the whole thing in another mind.

This gives the skill its posture. A rule is never taught as a list to memorise. It is taught as a
**solution to a problem of meaning**, and the list is the shape of that solution.

## The standing epigraph — meaning has two jobs in this guide

Meaning does two distinct jobs across the guides, and the skill must keep both straight:

1. **The PRINCIPLE** — a single standing claim about the whole enterprise, true of every heading at
   once. Rendered VERBATIM, ONCE, near the top of each guide (skill.md STEP 6/7) — never repeated
   per heading, never paraphrased run to run:

   > **Grammar allows the communication of meaning.**

   `Theatre.html` may dress this line in the metaphor (STEP 7's usual license); `Technical_Outline.
   html` renders it exactly as above. Either way it says the same thing once, and never again.

2. **The RULE'S OWN WHY** — specific to one rule, different rule to rule, even within the same
   heading. This is sentence A2 of that rule's DEFINITION (skill.md STEP 5's "FOR EACH RULE"), never
   a separate block of its own. It answers "why THIS rule", not "why grammar" — the PRINCIPLE above
   already covers the second question, once, for the whole guide.

The PRINCIPLE is what the whole exercise is FOR; a rule's A2 is what THIS rule is for. Confusing the
two produces either a guide with no unifying claim (every heading's why in isolation) or a guide
that repeats the same abstract claim under every heading instead of doing the specific work A2 is
for. Both fail — a heading with A2 that only restates the PRINCIPLE in different words has not done
its own work and should be rewritten until it says something true of THIS rule specifically.

## The seven principles

### 1. Intention precedes form

The speaker means something; the structure is how they get it across. So every analysis asks what
was meant **before** it asks what shape the words are in.

This is the principle behind head-finding. A structural test tells you which word the others hang
off. It does not tell you what the sentence is about — and what it is about is what the speaker
*chose* as the head.

> *a cup of coffee* — the head is **cup**. The sentence is about a vessel; the coffee says which kind.
> *a coffee cup* — the head is still **cup**, but now *coffee* has become part of what the cup **is**.
> *a coffee, in a cup* — the head is **coffee**. The cup has dropped to circumstance.

Same three words. Three different things meant. The structure followed the intention each time, not
the other way round.

* The head is the word the rest of the phrase exists to serve. Find what is meant; the head is what
  it is meant **about**.

> What is this sentence about? Take that answer away — does anything left still stand on its own?

### 2. Proximity tracks essence

**The nearer a modifier stands to its noun, the more nearly it belongs to what the thing is.**

This is the whole of adjective order, and it is not a convention. It is a ladder from the most
contingent to the most essential:

| Slot | Kind of adjective | How closely it belongs to the thing |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | opinion — *lovely, dreadful* | Not in the thing at all. It is in me. |
| 2 | size — *little, enormous* | In the thing, but it could change tomorrow. |
| 3 | age — *old, new* | In the thing, and changing whether I like it or not. |
| 4 | shape — *rectangular, round* | In the thing, and stable. |
| 5 | colour — *green, black* | In the thing's surface. |
| 6 | origin — *French, Cornish* | Where it came from — part of its history, not its surface. |
| 7 | material — *silver, oak* | What it is made of. Take this away and nothing is left. |
| 8 | purpose — *whittling, dining* | What it is **for**. For a made thing, this is what it is. |

> *a lovely little old rectangular green French silver whittling knife*

Read the slots outward from the noun and you are walking away from the thing's being and toward
your own opinion of it. Read them inward and you are walking from me, to it, to what it is made of,
to what it is for. *Silver* cannot be moved leftward past *French* without the phrase going wrong,
because being made of silver is nearer to being a knife than being made in France is.

The last slot is the one that makes the point. **Purpose sits closest of all** — closer even than
material — because for a thing that was made, what it is for is not a fact about it. It is the
reason it exists at all. A whittling knife that cannot whittle has stopped being a whittling knife,
while a silver one melted down has only stopped being silver.

* Modifiers stack outward from the essential to the accidental. If two orders are both grammatical,
  they mean different things — the inner one is being claimed as more nearly part of the thing.

> Move the word one place left. Does the phrase now claim something slightly different about what
> the thing **is**? If yes, the order was carrying meaning, not convention.

### 3. The same ladder runs through the verb

Adverb order is the adjective ladder again, pointed at an action instead of a thing. **Manner,
then place, then time.**

> *She sang beautifully in the hall last night.*

*Beautifully* is part of the singing — remove it and a different act happened. *In the hall* is
where the act sat. *Last night* is the frame the whole thing sat inside. Each step outward is one
step further from the action itself and one step nearer to the world around it.

So the nesting is real, not stylistic:

```
        [ last night                                   ]   ← the frame
             [ in the hall                        ]         ← the setting
                  [ beautifully · sang       ]              ← the act itself
```

* Manner belongs to the act; place and time belong to the world the act happened in. Order them
  from the act outward.

> Which of these could I remove and still have the same action take place? That one is furthest out.

### 4. Marked order means marked meaning

English has a default order. Departing from it is never free — the departure **is** the message.

> *Never have I seen such a thing.*

Nothing is added to the plain version. What changed is that *never* was moved to the front, and
fronting a word makes it the thing the hearer meets first. The emphasis is not decoration laid over
the sentence; it is the order itself.

* Where a sentence departs from default order, the departure carries the emphasis. Ask what was
  moved and what it was moved past.

> Put it back in the ordinary order. What is lost? That is what the movement was for.

### 5. Inflection is meaning made visible

An ending is not decoration and not history. It is a role in the meaning, written on the word so it
can be recognised out of position.

*Who* / *whom*, *he* / *him*, *walk* / *walked* — each pair marks a difference in what the word is
doing, and English keeps exactly those markings it still needs to avoid confusion. Where word order
already makes the role obvious, the marking has tended to fall away. Where it does not, the marking
survives. English lost most of its case endings precisely as its word order hardened; the two are
the same fact seen twice.

* An inflection persists only where meaning would otherwise be ambiguous. Where you find one, ask
  what confusion it is preventing.

> If this ending vanished, would the sentence still say the same thing?

### 6. Function outranks form

A word class is what a word is **doing here**, not what it is in the dictionary.

> *Running* is good for you. — a noun, whatever its shape says.
> The *running* water. — an adjective.
> He is *running*. — a verb.

Nothing changed in the word. What changed is the job it was given. Naming word classes off the
dictionary rather than off the job is the commonest way a parse goes wrong.

* Classify by what a word does in this sentence. The dictionary entry is a starting guess, not an
  answer.

> What job is this word doing here? Could another word of a different shape do exactly that job?

### 7. Structure is nested, not sequential

A sentence looks like a line and is not one. It is boxes inside boxes, flattened onto a line
because speech has no other option. Every bracketing question — clause boundaries, what a modifier
attaches to, where an ambiguity lives — is the question of which box a thing is in.

> *I saw the man with the telescope.*

Two meanings, one word order, because *with the telescope* can sit in either of two boxes:

```
[ I saw [ the man with the telescope ] ]     the man had it
[ I [ saw the man ] with the telescope ]     I used it
```

Ambiguity is not a defect of the sentence. It is the price of flattening a nested thing onto a line,
and it is resolved by asking what was meant — which returns to principle 1.

* When a sentence is ambiguous, do not choose. Show both boxes and say which reading each gives.

> What does this modifier belong to? Draw the box. Does the sentence still mean what it meant?

## How the frame uses a source

The four research sources (see `sources.md`) are consulted for coverage, conventional labels,
exceptions, and worked material. They are never quoted as authority for **why**.

```
  SOURCE SAYS                              FRAME DOES
  ─────────────────────────────────────    ──────────────────────────────────────────
  a rule, with a reason                →   check the reason against the seven
                                           principles; keep it if it agrees
  a rule, flatly, as convention        →   supply the meaning-based reason from the
  ("that's just the order")                principles — this is the skill's main job
  a rule, with a reason that            →   keep the RULE, discard the reason, write
  contradicts the frame                     the frame's own; never argue with the
                                            source in the rendered text
  a rule the frame cannot explain      →   FLAG it in the draft for Luke. Never
                                            invent a metaphysical reason to fill a
                                            hole — a fabricated why is worse than a
                                            named gap
  a label the frame would name          →   use the conventional label; the frame
  differently                               governs explanation, not vocabulary
```

The last two rows are the guardrails. The frame is a way of understanding grammar, not a licence to
make things up, and it never quarrels with a source on the page — the reader sees one coherent
account, not a dispute.

## What the reader sees

Every heading in both guides carries **one short passage giving the meaning-based reason** for the
rule it states — plainly, in `!SimpleEnglish`, without the words *metaphysical*, *ontological*,
*essence*, *principle*, or any reference to this file. The reasoning shows; the machinery does not.

> Yes: "Silver sits closer to the noun than French because what a knife is made of is nearer to
> being a knife than where it was made."
>
> No: "Adjective order reflects an ontological hierarchy of essential versus accidental properties."

Both say the same thing. Only the first one teaches.
