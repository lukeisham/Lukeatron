# sources.md — the research set

The deterministic half of `!GrammarFrame`'s research pass. These five sites are the **only**
grammar references consulted, plus the open web for illustrative quotations. The list is closed:
a sixth grammar source is added here first, with a reason, never reached for mid-run. Two more
sets of sites are named for narrower jobs and supply no grammar: **finding and verifying
quotations** (below) and **the VCAA glossary**, which supplies a label only (below).

All research happens at **generation time**. The rendered guides carry no scripts, no fonts, no
images and no links to any of these sites — they open from disk with the network off. That is
a hard requirement (criterion A8). The one exception is a plain hyperlink, not a load: D16's VCAA
term in `Technical_Outline.html`, which does nothing until it is clicked.

## The five

| # | Source | What it is good for | Its bias — what to correct for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | **eltconcourse.com** — Functional grammar introduction | The closest ally of the frame. Treats language as meaning-making and asks what a structure is *for* before asking what shape it is. Reach for it first on any "why is it built this way" question, and on clause function, theme/rheme, and process types. | Functional terminology can drift from the conventional labels Luke teaches. Take the reasoning, keep the traditional label. |
| 2 | **learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar** | Careful, CEFR-graded statements of what standard British English actually does. The best plain statement of a rule, and the safest source for what counts as standard. Good exception lists. | States rules as convention with no reason given — "this is the order we use." Every rule taken from here needs the frame's *why* supplied. |
| 3 | **perfect-english-grammar.com** | Clear learner-facing rules with contrasting example pairs. Strong on the tense system, conditionals, and articles. Its minimal pairs are useful raw material for worked examples. | Learner-simplified. A rule here is often a usable approximation with the hard cases trimmed off. Check any edge case against source 2 before stating it. |
| 4 | **owl.purdue.edu** | Mechanics, punctuation, sentence-level error patterns (comma splices, fragments, agreement, parallelism) — the things that come up when marking essays. Academic register. | American conventions, especially punctuation and some usage. Where it differs from British practice, follow source 2 and say nothing about the difference. |
| 5 | **The Internet Grammar of English** — UCL Survey of English Usage. The live site is down; read from the Internet Archive copy of 17 April 2025 (route below) | A free university course: word classes (nouns, determiners, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, minor classes), phrases, clauses, sentences, then functions (subject and predicate, inside the predicate, adjuncts, sentence patterns, functions in phrases). Reach for it to generate and to check how a structure is analysed — its parts and its job in the clause — and to draft the fuller technical account a heading needs. | Written for undergraduates in a linguist's vocabulary, which differs from some traditional labels: it counts *the*, *a* and *an* as kinds of **determiner**, and calls the optional how/when/where elements **adjuncts**. Take the description, keep Luke's label. A frozen copy — right as of 2025, never updated. **Read on 2026-09-26 (determiners, phrases, adjuncts).** |

**Precedence when two disagree on a fact:** British Council → Internet Grammar of English →
eltconcourse → Purdue OWL → perfect-english-grammar. On *reasoning* rather than fact,
eltconcourse leads and the frame (`meaning-first.md`) outranks all five.

## Illustrative quotations — finding and verifying

The memorable worked example under each heading is a real quotation, chosen because it is
recognisable and because it shows the structure cleanly.

**Where to look first.** These four. The wider open web is still open to a quotation, but a line
found there clears the same check below.

| Site | Good for | Watch for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **gutenberg.org** | Full public-domain texts — novels, essays, speeches, poems | Volunteer transcriptions of varying editions. One wrong word ruins a quotation, so where the wording turns on a single word, read a second edition. |
| **standardebooks.org** | The same kind of text in carefully proofread modern editions | Fewer titles. |
| **wikisource.org** | Texts with the scanned page beside the transcription | Only a page marked proofread or validated counts as a text. Any other page is a lead, not a check. |
| **biblegateway.com** | The Bible, King James Version by default | One translation per quotation; never splice two. |

```
  FIND     a recognisable line that genuinely exhibits the structure
             │
  VERIFY   exact wording AND attribution
             │
             ├── a line from a WORK — novel, poem, essay, Bible verse
             │     the text is the check: read the line in a full edition on one of the four
             │     sites; author and work (or book, chapter and verse) come from that edition
             │
             ├── a SAYING or a SPEECH line — the "who said this?" kind
             │     Quote Investigator (quoteinvestigator.com) must have an entry crediting the
             │     same person with the same wording — the entry itself, not a search snippet
             │
             ├── verified ──────→  use it, with author + work shown inline
             │
             └── cannot verify ─→  DISCARD and find another.
                                   Never publish an unverified quote under a real author's name.
```

Quote Investigator covers only the quotations someone has researched — a few thousand — so it is
the check for sayings, not a database of everything. A saying it does not cover cannot be
verified, and is discarded. It is not needed for a line taken from a text edition.

Quote-aggregator sites (BrainyQuote, Goodreads, AZQuotes and similar) are **not** verification.
They may be used to find a candidate; they can never confirm one.

**Verification is autonomous.** Luke is never asked to confirm a quote, an attribution, or a
grammar fact. An unverifiable quote is discarded and replaced without comment; what the checking
found is reported in the run summary, not raised as a question. Acting on what verification finds
about *Luke's own content* is a different matter — that is a correction, and corrections are
always brought to the gate (see the skill's TWO LANES section).

**How a verified quote is rendered:** the quotation, then author and work inline — e.g.
*— Austen, Pride and Prejudice*. That is the whole of it. No MLA apparatus, no footnote, no URL,
no bibliography. This is a deliberate exception to the house citation convention, because these
guides are Luke's own synthesis rather than a sourced reference. Attribution is shown so a quote
can be checked by eye; a citation is not, because the document does not argue from authority.

For structures no famous line happens to exhibit, a plainly-attributed line from a well-known
public text (a hymn, a speech, a King James verse, a nursery rhyme) is preferred over inventing a
sentence — but an invented sentence with no attribution is better than a misattributed real one.

## The curriculum label — VCAA English glossary

**What it is.** The Victorian Curriculum F–10 English glossary (Version 2.0), the term set Victorian
schools use. Landing page — the address every rendered link points to:
`https://f10.vcaa.vic.edu.au/learning-areas/english/english/glossary`. The glossary is offered there
as a Word download, not as web pages, so no single term has an address of its own; the link lands on
the page, and the reader finds the term in the file.

**What it is for — a label, and only a label.** Beside a technical term the guide shows what Victorian
schools call the same concept, so Luke sees both names together. It never supplies a fact, a rule, a
reason or a diagnostic. It never outranks any source. It never changes Luke's term or the guide's own:
where the two labels differ, both stand.

```
  LOOK UP   each term the heading adds to the guide's Glossary appendix (D2) in the VCAA glossary
              │
              ├── an entry is HEADED with the same concept
              │     ──→ record that heading
              │
              ├── the glossary defines it INSIDE another entry, or gives it as an
              │   alternative name (embedded clause, inside Clause; adjunct, inside Adverbial)
              │     ──→ record the term as that entry writes it
              │
              ├── only a NEAR match, and Luke has ruled it equal (table below)
              │     ──→ record the term he ruled
              │
              └── no entry, not sure, or a near match he has not ruled ──→ record nothing.
                    A wrong label is worse than none. Never guess.
              (same concept = the two definitions describe the same thing —
               not that the words look alike)
  SHOW      per D16 — Technical_Outline.html only, in the term's Glossary entry only
```

**Reading the file.** The plain fetch tool returns nothing for a Word file. Use `curl` and read the
text in memory, writing nothing to disk (A12) — a `.docx` is a zip archive whose text is in
`word/document.xml`. Take the file's current address from the landing page. On 2026-09-26 it was
`https://f10.vcaa.vic.edu.au/sites/default/files/2025-09/English%20glossary_13-12-2023.docx`
(144,388 bytes). Luke approved the download that day. This snippet was run then and printed all 179
term headings cleanly:

```
curl -sL "<the file's address>" | python3 -c '
import sys, io, zipfile, xml.etree.ElementTree as ET
W = "{http://schemas.openxmlformats.org/wordprocessingml/2006/main}"
root = ET.fromstring(zipfile.ZipFile(io.BytesIO(sys.stdin.buffer.read())).read("word/document.xml"))
for p in root.iter(W + "p"):
    s = p.find(f"{W}pPr/{W}pStyle")
    t = "".join(x.text or "" for x in p.iter(W + "t")).strip()
    if t: print(("\n## " if s is not None and s.get(W + "val") == "GlossaryTerm" else "") + t)
'
```

Use a real XML parser, not a text search: a search for `<w:t` also catches `<w:tab>` tags and fills the
text with XML.

**What reading it showed (2026-09-26).** 179 terms. Each is a paragraph styled `GlossaryTerm`, its
definition the paragraphs after it.

- **Match the concept, never the words.** *Subject matter* and *Subjective language* are not
  "subject"; *Objective language* is not "object". The glossary has no entry of its own for the
  grammatical object, participle, infinitive, relative clause or adjective group. Determiner and
  complement appear only in passing.
- **Alternatives sit inside the text**, so a phrase search misses them: "independent (or main) clause",
  "dependent (or subordinate) clause", "auxiliary (or helping) verbs". A few headings carry one too:
  "Genre (or literary genre)".
- **Some concepts are defined inside another entry:** embedded clause and predicate inside *Clause*;
  noun phrase and verb phrase inside *Phrase*; adjunct as an alternative name inside *Adverbial*.
- **Headings can be plural:** "Noun groups", "Verb groups", "Prepositional phrases", "Coordinating
  conjunctions". D16 shows a term as its entry writes it.
- **A near match is not a match.** The glossary has BOTH "noun phrase" (inside *Phrase*: a phrase with
  a noun as head) AND, separately, *Noun groups*. It never says they are the same. So a near match
  shows in the bracket only where Luke has ruled it (below); any other near match is named in the run
  summary so he can rule on it.

**Luke's rulings on near matches.** A row is added only when he rules. It pairs two labels for the
bracket; it does not say the two concepts are the same in every respect.

| Guide term | Bracket shows | Ruled | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| noun phrase | noun groups | 2026-09-26, Luke | The glossary's *Noun groups* describes only what comes before the head noun. A noun phrase can also carry material after it ("the men who died", in the glossary's own *Phrase* entry). |
| verb phrase | verb groups | 2026-09-26, Luke | The glossary's *Phrase* entry also uses the term "verb phrase" ("went", "had gone"). |

## When a source can't be reached

Several of these sites block automated fetching, and any of them can be down.

```
  1  The plain fetch is refused (HTTP 403, a challenge page, or archive.org)
       ⇒ read it another way: the browser route (!HeadlessChromeBrowser) for a live page,
         plain `curl` for archive.org
  2  Still unreachable
       ⇒ the source is UNAVAILABLE this run. Carry on with the others, in precedence.
         Do NOT flag every heading. Name the source ONCE, in the run summary to Luke, with the
         date and what was tried.
  3  A fact only the unavailable source could have settled (the others disagree or are silent)
       ⇒ that heading takes a flag, as for any unresolved disagreement between sources (C17)
```

**The Internet Grammar of English — read from the Internet Archive.** On 2026-09-26 the live site was
down: `ucl.ac.uk/internet-grammar/` returned a 502 in a real browser, `ucl.ac.uk` refuses the plain
fetch (403, behind Cloudflare), and the older `ucl.ac.uk/english-usage/resources/ige/` address
redirects to the Survey's home page, which no longer lists it. Luke approved reading the Internet
Archive's copy of 17 April 2025 instead. Plain `curl` reads it; the plain fetch tool refuses
archive.org. The route, as traced and tested that day:

```
BASE   https://web.archive.org/web/20250417005003/https://www.ucl.ac.uk/internet-grammar/

  contents/contbody.htm     the course's 20 sections, in order
  <name>/<name>.htm         a word-class, phrase or clause section — a frame shell only. Names:
                            intro wordclas nouns determin verbs adjectiv adverbs preps conjunct
                            minor phrases clauses
    └─ its content frame    read the shell's <frame src>: e.g. determin/xdetm1.htm, phrases/xphrs1.htm.
                            A section runs over several pages ("page 1/3").
  Functions sections        contents/<name>.htm → function/<name>.htm → function/xfuncN.htm
                            (names: function subjpred inpred adjunct sentpatt).
                            Adjuncts is function/xfunc7.htm, "page 7/9".
```

Read that day: determiners, phrases and adjuncts. The other sections should follow the same pattern
but were not opened. A section's address gives the Archive's nearest capture, so the pages carry
different dates (the shells seen were captured between 2021 and 2025). The copy is a stand-in, and
the run summary says when it was used.

## Scope of the research pass

**In scope.** Filling a heading Luke pasted: a rule he stated loosely, a missing diagnostic, an
exception he did not mention, a table of a closed set, a worked example. Proposing a *new* heading
adjacent to what he pasted — proposed only, never added without his sign-off at the gate.

**Out of scope.** Rewriting Luke's own wording in `Original_Content.md`; importing a source's
explanatory framing wholesale; teaching a topic the paste does not touch; anything that would make
a rendered file depend on the network at read time.
