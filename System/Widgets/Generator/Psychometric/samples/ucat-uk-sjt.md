# UCAT — Situational Judgement Test Subtest — UK

**Reference test:** UCAT — Situational Judgement Test subtest (UK)
**Format:** 69 scenarios, 26 minutes. Each scenario followed by 4 response options. Rate each as: Very appropriate, Appropriate, Inappropriate but not awful, Very inappropriate — or rank in order of appropriateness.
**Answer style:** Partial-credit (correct ratings are ±1 tolerance from key; rankings use Kendall's tau).
**Context:** All scenarios are set in medical, dental, or clinical education environments.

---

## UCAT SJT Scenario — Medical Student Witnesses Peer Cheating

**Difficulty:** Hard
**Format:** Rate each response (Very appropriate / Appropriate / Inappropriate but not awful / Very inappropriate)

### Stimulus

You are a third-year medical student on clinical placement. While waiting to use a shared computer in the hospital library, you notice that another medical student, Priya, has left her online examination window open. You can see that she has been accessing revision notes during a timed formative assessment that explicitly prohibits external resources. You and Priya are in the same problem-based learning group and have collaborated well throughout the year. The assessment represents 10% of the module grade.

### Question

How **appropriate** is each of the following responses?

### Options

- **A:** Close the browser window so no one else sees it, and say nothing to Priya. You don't want to cause trouble.
- **B:** Tell Priya privately what you saw and encourage her to report herself to the module coordinator.
- **C:** Report what you saw to the module coordinator without speaking to Priya first.
- **D:** Speak to your clinical tutor confidentially, explaining what you witnessed and asking for guidance on how to proceed.

### Correct Answer Key

| Response | Rating |
|---|---|
| **A** | Very inappropriate |
| **B** | Appropriate |
| **C** | Inappropriate but not awful |
| **D** | Very appropriate |

### Clue

Medical schools have fitness-to-practise procedures. Consider the difference between covering up, confronting directly, reporting formally, and seeking confidential guidance.

### Explainer

**D (Very appropriate):** Seeking confidential guidance from a clinical tutor respects both Priya's dignity and the institution's integrity procedures. It elevates the concern through the proper channel without jumping to formal reporting before you have all the facts. This demonstrates integrity while recognising that you may not have full context (e.g., perhaps the exam window was open for a legitimate reason).

**B (Appropriate):** Speaking to Priya directly is collegial and gives her the opportunity to take responsibility herself. However, it places the burden on you to follow up and carries some risk that the behaviour continues unaddressed if she does not self-report.

**C (Inappropriate but not awful):** Directly reporting to the module coordinator bypasses collegial discussion with Priya and may escalate a situation that could be resolved informally. However, formal reporting is not inherently wrong — it's the skipping of intermediate steps that makes it less appropriate.

**A (Very inappropriate):** Covering up evidence (closing the browser) and saying nothing constitutes complicity. This breaches professional integrity standards and the duty of candour expected of medical students. Fitness-to-practise panels would view this very seriously.
