# UCAT — Verbal Reasoning Subtest — UK

**Reference test:** UCAT — Verbal Reasoning subtest (UK)
**Format:** 11 passages (200–400 words each), 44 questions, 21 minutes. For each passage, 4 statements to classify as True, False, or Can't Tell based on the passage content.
**Answer style:** True / False / Can't Tell. Binary scoring. No partial credit.
**Context:** Passages drawn from medicine, science, social policy, history, and current affairs.

## UCAT VR Passage — Antibiotic Resistance

**Difficulty:** Medium

### Stimulus

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has been identified by the World Health Organization as one of the top ten global public health threats. In 2019, an estimated 1.27 million deaths were directly attributable to bacterial AMR, with the highest burden falling on sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. The primary driver of AMR is the overuse and misuse of antibiotics in both human medicine and agriculture. In many low- and middle-income countries, antibiotics are available over the counter without prescription, enabling self-medication for viral infections against which antibiotics are ineffective.

In the United Kingdom, antibiotic prescribing in primary care fell by 17% between 2014 and 2019, following a nationwide antimicrobial stewardship programme. However, hospital prescribing rates remained largely unchanged over the same period. The UK government's 2019–2024 national action plan set a target of reducing human antibiotic use by a further 15% by 2024, though the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted surveillance and reporting systems, making it difficult to assess whether this target was met. The plan also committed £32 million to AMR research.

Globally, the development of new antibiotics has slowed dramatically since the 1980s. Only two new classes of antibiotics have reached the market in the last three decades. Pharmaceutical companies have increasingly deprioritised antibiotic research due to low return on investment: antibiotics are typically taken for short courses, and new drugs are often held in reserve to preserve their effectiveness, limiting sales volume. In contrast, chronic-disease medications generate reliable long-term revenue. Several governments have introduced "subscription-style" payment models that compensate pharmaceutical companies for maintaining antibiotic availability regardless of sales volume, a policy innovation designed to re-align commercial incentives with public health needs.

### Questions

For each statement, select **True** (supported by the passage), **False** (contradicted by the passage), or **Can't Tell** (insufficient information in the passage to determine).

**1.** The majority of global deaths attributable to bacterial AMR in 2019 occurred in high-income countries.

**Answer:** **False**

**Explanation:** The passage states "the highest burden falling on sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia," which directly contradicts the claim that the majority occurred in high-income countries.

---

**2.** In the UK, fewer antibiotics were prescribed in hospitals in 2019 than in 2014.

**Answer:** **Can't Tell**

**Explanation:** The passage states that hospital prescribing rates "remained largely unchanged," but this refers to the *rate* of prescribing, not the absolute number. It is possible that the absolute number changed (due to changes in hospital admissions, for example) even if the rate remained stable. The passage does not provide data on absolute numbers of hospital prescriptions.

---

**3.** The UK government's 2019–2024 AMR action plan included a financial commitment to research.

**Answer:** **True**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly states "The plan also committed £32 million to AMR research."

---

**4.** Pharmaceutical companies have invested less in antibiotic development than in chronic-disease medications primarily because of stricter regulatory requirements for antibiotics.

**Answer:** **False**

**Explanation:** The passage states that the reason is "low return on investment" due to short courses and drugs being held in reserve, not regulatory requirements. The passage gives a different reason than the one claimed in the statement.

---

### Clue

Read carefully for what is **explicitly stated** vs. what is **inferred or assumed**. "Can't Tell" is a valid answer when the passage provides some related information but not enough to confirm or contradict the specific claim in the statement.

### Explainer

UCAT Verbal Reasoning is a test of careful reading, not general knowledge. The key skill is distinguishing between: (1) what the passage actually says (True), (2) what the passage says the opposite of (False), and (3) what you might assume or know from elsewhere but the passage does not confirm (Can't Tell). Common traps: plausible-sounding claims that the passage didn't mention, numeric claims that use different metrics than the passage (e.g., absolute numbers vs. rates), and statements that are true in the real world but not stated in the passage.
