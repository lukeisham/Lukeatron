**id:** aesop-002
**title:** The Lion and the Mouse
**culture/region:** Ancient Greece
**tale_text:**

The lion seized the frightened little creature and was about to swallow him when the mouse cried out, "Please, mighty lion, let me go! If you do, I promise I shall never forget your kindness. Perhaps one day I may be able to return the favor." The lion, amused by this promise, let the mouse go. Some time later, the lion was caught in a hunter's net. As he roared in rage and struggled futilely, the little mouse heard his voice. She ran to the net and began gnawing the ropes with her teeth, working as hard as she could. Soon the lion was free! "You see," said the mouse, "even a mouse can help a lion."

## BEAT-DETECTION GUIDANCE FOR LOGIC ENGINE

The following signals and patterns help a rules-based JavaScript system identify the three narrative beats in a folk tale.

### SETUP (Opening Frame / Context)

**Linguistic markers:**
- Temporal connectives: "Once upon a time", "In ancient times", "One day", "There once was", "A long time ago", "In a distant land"
- Existential markers: "There lived", "There was", "Once there was", "A man/woman had", "A [character] owned"
- Scene-setting clauses: Passive voice describing initial conditions ("A princess was born...", "A farmer lived...")
- Adjectives establishing normalcy: "a poor", "a wealthy", "a young", "an old", "a beautiful", "a humble"

**Grammatical patterns:**
- Simple past tense establishing prior state
- Agent + object introduction (introducing main character and their condition)
- Spatial/temporal framing (location and time period)
- Character description in past tense

**Discourse markers:**
- First mention of main character(s)
- First statement of the problem/situation that drives action
- Establishment of emotional stakes (desire, lack, imbalance)
- No action-verbs indicating change yet

**Common beat length:** 1-3 sentences for short folk tales

---

### TWIST or TURN (Complication / Reversal)

**Linguistic markers:**
- Causal/adversative connectives: "But", "However", "Yet", "When", "One day", "Suddenly", "Just then", "Meanwhile", "Alas", "Unexpectedly"
- Discourse markers of change: "and then", "thereupon", "forthwith", "presently"
- Verbs signaling action/change: "decided", "discovered", "encountered", "appeared", "demanded", "refused", "seized", "began", "attempted"
- Negation markers: "could not", "refused", "denied", "failed", "was unable"

**Grammatical patterns:**
- Shift from simple past (describing state) to past perfect or narrative past (describing event)
- Introduction of antagonistic force or obstacle
- Change in verb aspect from stative ("was") to dynamic ("did", "acted")
- Conditional or hypothetical structures ("if", "though", "as if")

**Discourse markers:**
- Shift in grammatical subject (introducing new agent or force affecting the protagonist)
- Increase in sentence complexity (compound sentences with conjunctions)
- Introduction of dialogue or direct speech (characters' reactions or demands)
- Departure from expected outcome hinted in setup

**Common beat length:** 1-5 sentences; may contain sub-beats for multi-stage complications

**Typical positions:** Middle section of tale; occurs after setup establishes context

---

### RESULT or CONSEQUENCE (Resolution / Outcome)

**Linguistic markers:**
- Conclusive connectives: "So", "Thus", "Therefore", "In the end", "At last", "Finally", "Soon", "And so", "From that day on", "Ever after"
- Evaluative language: "learned", "understood", "realized", "proved", "showed", "demonstrated"
- Moral closure markers: "the moral is", "this teaches that", "and they lived", "the lesson was", "he became", "she never again"
- Finality markers: "never again", "forevermore", "to this day", "and lived happily", "the end"

**Grammatical patterns:**
- Shift to simple past describing outcome state (parallel to setup's stative verbs, but now describing new equilibrium)
- Subject of result sentence often same as setup subject (closure of character arc)
- Passive voice describing rewards or punishments ("was rewarded", "was punished", "was transformed")
- Potential modal verbs: "could now", "was able to", "would never"

**Discourse markers:**
- Resolution of the problem/conflict introduced in SETUP
- Return to normalcy or establishment of new normalcy
- Explicit statement of consequence for protagonist and/or antagonist
- Echo or reversal of opening condition (thematic closure)

**Common beat length:** 1-4 sentences; often shorter than TWIST

**Typical positions:** Final section of tale; marks the return to equilibrium after complication

---

### SIGNAL PATTERN COMBINATIONS

**High-confidence SETUP signals:**
- Temporal opener + existential marker + character descriptor
- Example: "Once upon a time, there lived an old woman who was very poor."

**High-confidence TWIST signals:**
- Adversative connective + action verb + obstacle/change
- Example: "But one day, a stranger appeared at her door with an impossible demand."

**High-confidence RESULT signals:**
- Conclusive connective + evaluation/moral + state change
- Example: "Thus the old woman learned the price of greed and lived in poverty ever after."

**Ambiguity resolution** (when signals overlap):
- Check adjacency: if two signals appear in same sentence, the sentence likely belongs to the same beat
- Check consistency: SETUP verbs are stative; TWIST verbs are dynamic; RESULT verbs return to stative describing outcome
- Check narrative logic: SETUP precedes the complication; TWIST introduces change; RESULT follows logically from twist

**Edge cases:**
- Tales with embedded stories: Apply beat logic to the frame story first, then to embedded narrative
- Multiple twists: Mark the PRIMARY twist that most directly opposes the setup condition; secondary complications may blur beats
- Ambiguous closures: If a tale ends without explicit resolution (common in trickster tales), mark the consequences observable in final action
