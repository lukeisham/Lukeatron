## Category 1: Situational Judgement Tests (SJTs)

SJTs present workplace scenarios and ask candidates to rate or rank possible responses. No single "correct" answer exists—responses are evaluated against organisational values and competencies. Format: **Rank or Rate** (Most → Least appropriate; or Rate 1–5).

### Item SJT-01
**id:** SJT-01
**category:** Situational Judgement Tests
**stimulus:** You are leading a project team. A senior colleague publicly contradicts your decision in a team meeting, suggesting your approach will fail. Several team members look uncomfortable. You have time to respond.
**question:** How would you rank these responses from **most to least** appropriate?
**options:**
- A: Defend your position strongly, pointing out errors in their analysis to preserve credibility.
- B: Acknowledge their concern, invite them to discuss it privately after the meeting.
- C: Suggest the team vote on the best approach.
- D: Ask them to present their full alternative plan to the team immediately.
- E: Thank them for the input and move forward with your original plan unchanged.
**correct_answer:** B, D, A, E, C
**clue:** Consider emotional safety, professional respect, and decision-making authority. Which response prevents escalation while addressing the core issue?
**explainer:** B is most appropriate—it validates the concern, removes public tension, and shows confidence while creating space for dialogue. D comes next (getting full alternatives on the table demonstrates inclusivity). A addresses credibility but risks defensiveness. E appears confident but dismisses valid input. C abdicates leadership. The ranking reflects emotional intelligence + respect for hierarchy.
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item SJT-02
**id:** SJT-02
**category:** Situational Judgement Tests
**stimulus:** You discover a colleague has submitted work with data that appears to have been fabricated. You have no direct proof, only statistical implausibility. Your colleague is well-liked and has been at the organisation longer than you.
**question:** Rate each response on a scale of 1 (most appropriate) to 5 (least appropriate):
**options:**
- A: Consult your manager confidentially before taking any action.
- B: Ask the colleague directly about the data sources and methodology.
- C: Cross-check the data independently and document your findings.
- D: Report the concern anonymously to the ethics hotline.
- E: Let it go; it's not your responsibility.
**correct_answer:** A:1, C:2, B:3, D:4, E:5
**clue:** Integrity matters, but so does due diligence and chain of command. What's the ethical path that respects both?
**explainer:** A is most appropriate—consulting your manager ensures proper handling and protects both you and the colleague. C is responsible (independent verification builds your case). B risks confrontation if your suspicion is wrong. D (anonymous reporting) bypasses chain of command unless other channels fail. E is never acceptable in integrity matters. The scale reflects escalation: internal + documented > direct conversation > formal complaint > avoidance.
**difficulty:** Hard

### Item SJT-03
**id:** SJT-03
**category:** Situational Judgement Tests
**stimulus:** Your team has a Friday afternoon deadline for a deliverable. It's 2pm Thursday and you realise the project timeline was based on a misunderstanding of client requirements. Fixing it properly will take an extra 8 hours. Your team is already stretched.
**question:** Rank these responses from **most to least** appropriate:
**options:**
- A: Deliver on time with the current misaligned solution; address gaps in a post-delivery review.
- B: Notify the client immediately about the timeline slip and negotiate a Monday deadline.
- C: Have your team work Thursday evening to meet Friday deadline.
- D: Deliver on time, but flag the requirement misalignment in your handover documentation.
- E: Ask the client which parts matter most and deliver a partial solution by Friday.
**correct_answer:** B, E, D, C, A
**clue:** Transparency, client value, and team sustainability all matter. What serves the client's true needs *and* protects the team?
**explainer:** B is best—early communication (24 hours notice) respects the client and allows renegotiation; most clients prefer knowing over surprises. E is responsible (partial delivery with triage respects time constraints while delivering value). D is defensive (flagging misalignment after delivery feels evasive). C burns team morale for a self-imposed deadline. A fails the client entirely. Ranked by: communication + client value > partial delivery > post-hoc flagging > unsustainable effort > misalignment delivery.
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item SJT-04
**id:** SJT-04
**category:** Situational Judgement Tests
**stimulus:** You're in a training session led by your director. You notice the content contains a significant factual error that contradicts established best practice. Several junior staff are present and taking notes. The session has 10 minutes remaining.
**question:** Rate from 1 (most appropriate) to 5 (least appropriate):
**options:**
- A: Raise your hand and politely correct the error during the session.
- B: Stay silent during the session; email the director privately afterward with your concern.
- C: Speak up, but phrase it as a question to soften the correction.
- D: Mention it casually to the junior staff afterward, explaining the correct approach.
- E: Say nothing; the director will catch it eventually.
**correct_answer:** C:1, B:2, A:3, D:4, E:5
**clue:** Respect hierarchy, but junior staff are learning. How do you protect accuracy *and* the director's credibility?
**explainer:** C is most appropriate—a question-framed correction avoids direct contradiction while preserving the director's authority. It also signals to junior staff that thinking critically is valued. B is responsible (private conversation shows respect) but delays correction when learners need it. A is direct but risks undermining authority. D is gossip-adjacent and confuses the junior staff. E fails your duty to accuracy. Ranked: tactful public correction > private follow-up > direct correction > confusing back-channel > avoidance.
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item SJT-05
**id:** SJT-05
**category:** Situational Judgement Tests

They are a single parent dealing with childcare issues, which you know from casual conversation. You are not their manager, but you work closely with them.

**options:**
- A: Pull them aside privately to discuss the impact on the team and ask what support might help.

- C: Reassign their tasks to other team members without discussing it with them.
- D: Mention the pattern publicly in the next team meeting to create accountability.

**explainer:** A is best—private conversation shows respect, acknowledges their context, and may surface barriers. It's also your role as a peer. E is next (informal heads-up to the manager prevents surprise and keeps process informal). B is appropriate (documenting creates a record) but less personal. C undermines trust and avoids the real issue. D is public shaming and escalates unnecessarily. Ranked: compassionate direct conversation > manager prep > formal documentation > circumventing the person > public accountability.
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item SJT-07

## Category 2: Verbal Critical Reasoning

Verbal Critical Reasoning tests assess your ability to analyse arguments, identify assumptions, detect logical flaws, and draw valid conclusions from text passages. Format: **Read passage, answer 1–2 multiple-choice questions** (True/False/Cannot Say, or best answer).

### Item VCR-01
**id:** VCR-01
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "The charity reported a 30% increase in donations last year. However, operational costs also rose by 25%. When adjusted for inflation, donations actually declined by 5% in real terms. Trustees attributed the nominal increase to a new social media campaign, but the timing doesn't match: the campaign launched in month 9 of the fiscal year, yet donation growth was strongest in months 1–3."
**question:** Which of the following is an **unstated assumption** in the Trustees' attribution of donation growth to the social media campaign?
**options:**
- A: The social media campaign was the only new initiative that year.
- B: Donations from the campaign would have appeared within weeks of launch.
- C: Adjusted-for-inflation donations are irrelevant to evaluating the campaign's success.
- D: The trustees had no other explanation for the month 1–3 donation spike.
- E: Real-terms decline in donations contradicts the campaign's value.
**correct_answer:** D
**clue:** The trustees attributed growth to the campaign. What must they be assuming to make that claim *despite the timing problem*?
**explainer:** D is unstated and necessary to their argument—if they had *another* explanation for the month 1–3 spike, they couldn't credit the campaign. A is too strong (campaigns aren't the only factor). B is reasonable but not unstated. C and E are about whether their measure is valid, not about their attribution logic. The key error: they've ignored timing and confused nominal vs. real growth.
**difficulty:** Hard

### Item VCR-02
**id:** VCR-02
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "Studies show that people who exercise regularly report higher life satisfaction than sedentary people. Therefore, regular exercise is the cause of higher life satisfaction."
**question:** Which of the following best identifies the **logical flaw** in this argument?
**options:**
- A: It confuses correlation with causation.
- B: It overgeneralises from a small sample.
- C: It ignores the cost of exercise time.
- D: It assumes all people have equal baseline satisfaction.
- E: It relies on self-reported data, which is unreliable.
**correct_answer:** A
**clue:** The argument moves from "people who exercise *tend to* report higher satisfaction" to "exercise *causes* higher satisfaction." What's the logical leap?
**explainer:** A is the core flaw—correlation (exercisers report higher satisfaction) is not causation (exercise causes the satisfaction). Reverse causation is possible (satisfied people exercise more), or a third variable (health, time management) might cause both. B is possible but the argument doesn't claim statistical significance. C, D, E are about data quality, not the logical structure of the inference.
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item VCR-03
**id:** VCR-03
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "Remote work has been shown to reduce office overhead costs by up to 40%. Critics worry that remote work reduces team cohesion and innovation. However, a survey of 500 remote workers found that 72% felt they were more productive at home than in the office. Therefore, organisations should adopt remote work policies."
**question:** Which statement would **most weaken** the argument for remote-work adoption?
**options:**
- A: Some industries may not be suitable for remote work.
- B: Overhead cost savings vary widely by company size.
- C: Productivity surveys are prone to bias because workers want to justify remote arrangements.
- D: Remote workers report better work-life balance.
- E: Office environments foster informal mentorship.
**correct_answer:** C
**clue:** The argument relies on a specific piece of evidence: worker surveys showing 72% higher productivity. What undermines *that evidence specifically*?
**explainer:** C is strongest—it questions the validity of the survey evidence itself (bias in self-reported productivity). If the survey is unreliable, the main empirical pillar of the argument collapses. A and B are limitations but don't weaken the logical inference. D actually supports the argument. E is a concern (noted by critics) but the argument addresses innovation separately.
**difficulty:** Hard

### Item VCR-04
**id:** VCR-04
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "All successful entrepreneurs are risk-takers. Jane is a successful entrepreneur. Therefore, Jane is a risk-taker."
**question:** Is this argument **logically valid**?
**options:**
- A: Yes; it follows the valid form of categorical syllogism.
- B: No; it commits the fallacy of affirming the consequent.
- C: No; it assumes Jane's success is permanent.
- D: Yes; but the premises may not be true.
- E: No; it confuses sufficient and necessary conditions.
**correct_answer:** A
**clue:** Check the logical form: All X are Y. Jane is X. Therefore, Jane is Y. Is this form valid, regardless of whether the premises are true?
**explainer:** A is correct—the syllogism is logically valid (if the premises were true, the conclusion must be true). B, C, E describe other fallacies, not this structure. D is a half-answer (yes it's valid, but doesn't address the question fully). The argument is *formally* valid but *materially* questionable—not all successful entrepreneurs are risk-takers.
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item VCR-05
**id:** VCR-05
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "The school's reading proficiency scores improved by 8% after the district purchased new tablets for classroom use. The superintendent announced that technology investment is the key to student success."
**question:** Which of the following, if true, would **most weaken** the superintendent's conclusion?
**options:**
- A: Tablets are more expensive than traditional textbooks.
- B: The 8% improvement is within the normal variation of yearly scores.
- C: Other schools without tablet programs also showed improvement that year.
- D: Teachers did not receive training on how to use the tablets effectively.
- E: Students spend more time on screens due to tablet use.
**correct_answer:** B
**clue:** The superintendent attributes the improvement to technology. What evidence would suggest the improvement wasn't due to the tablets at all?
**explainer:** B is strongest—if 8% is normal random variation (not statistically significant), then attributing it to tablets is unfounded. C is also strong (confounding variable: other factors improved scores district-wide), but B directly undermines the evidentiary claim. A is about cost, not efficacy. D and E are concerns but don't negate the observed improvement.
**difficulty:** Hard

### Item VCR-06
**id:** VCR-06
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "To be a doctor, one must complete medical school. Sarah is not in medical school. Therefore, Sarah is not a doctor."
**question:** Is this argument logically valid?
**options:**
- A: Yes; it's a valid form of modus tollens.
- B: Yes; it follows from the contrapositive of the original statement.
- C: No; Sarah could have already completed medical school.
- D: No; it confuses necessary and sufficient conditions.
- E: No; it assumes Sarah wants to be a doctor.
**correct_answer:** A
**clue:** The form is: If X, then Y. Not Y. Therefore, not X. Is this a valid logical form?
**explainer:** A is correct—this is **modus tollens**, a valid logical form (denying the consequent validly denies the antecedent). B is also technically correct (the contrapositive is logically equivalent), but A is the standard name. C misunderstands the argument (it doesn't claim Sarah never completed medical school—just that she's not currently in it). D and E describe other issues, not this logical structure.
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item VCR-07
**id:** VCR-07
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "Renewable energy is essential for climate stability. Wind turbines are a form of renewable energy. Some windmills harm bird populations. Therefore, wind turbines are bad for the environment overall."
**question:** Which statement best identifies the **logical problem** with this conclusion?
**options:**
- A: It fails to distinguish between wind turbines and windmills.
- B: It concludes that something is "bad overall" based on one negative aspect.
- C: It ignores the evidence that renewable energy is essential.
- D: It assumes bird population impact is worse than climate harm.
- E: It contradicts the premise that wind turbines are renewable energy.
**correct_answer:** B
**clue:** The argument notes one negative (bird harm) and concludes wind turbines are "bad overall." What's the logical flaw?
**explainer:** B is best—acknowledging a single negative aspect doesn't permit a sweeping "bad overall" conclusion without weighing costs vs. benefits. You'd need to compare bird harm against climate benefits. A is a quibble (wind turbines and windmills are functionally equivalent here). C actually strengthens the argument for wind. D is implicit in the argument but not the logical flaw. E is incorrect (wind turbines remain renewable energy).
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item VCR-08
**id:** VCR-08
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "Company X increased salaries by 5% last year and saw employee turnover drop by 12%. The CEO credits the raise. However, the same year, Company X introduced flexible work arrangements, hired a new wellness coach, and redesigned office spaces for open collaboration."
**question:** The argument that salary increases caused the drop in turnover is weakened by the fact that:
**options:**
- A: 5% may be below inflation rates in some regions.
- B: Other factors were introduced that could also have reduced turnover.
- C: Employee turnover is not the only measure of satisfaction.
- D: The CEO is biased toward believing in compensation's impact.
- E: Some employees may leave for reasons unrelated to their work environment.
**correct_answer:** B
**clue:** Multiple changes happened at once. How does this affect the CEO's claim about what caused the turnover drop?
**explainer:** B is strongest—it's a **confounding variable** problem. Multiple simultaneous changes make it impossible to isolate which caused the turnover improvement. A questions adequacy of the raise. C and E are fair points but don't weaken the *specific* claim. D is about bias, not logic.
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item VCR-09
**id:** VCR-09
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "Studies prove that breakfast improves academic performance. Students who eat breakfast have higher grades than those who skip it. Therefore, all students should eat breakfast."
**question:** Which assumption is **necessary** for the argument's conclusion to hold?
**options:**
- A: Breakfast must be nutritious to be beneficial.
- B: No other factors besides breakfast explain the grade difference.
- C: Skipping breakfast is the primary cause of low grades.
- D: All students have access to breakfast.
- E: Students who eat breakfast study more effectively.
**correct_answer:** B
**clue:** The argument infers causation from a correlation. What must be assumed about alternative explanations?
**explainer:** B is necessary—the argument assumes that breakfast *alone* explains the grade difference, not other variables (socioeconomic status, parental involvement, health). Without this assumption, recommending breakfast to all students is unfounded. A is a quality consideration (reasonable but not necessary). C overstates the claim. D and E are practical concerns but not logically necessary for the inference.
**difficulty:** Hard

### Item VCR-10
**id:** VCR-10
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "No reptiles are warm-blooded. All mammals are warm-blooded. Snakes are reptiles. Therefore, snakes are not mammals."
**question:** Is this argument logically valid?
**options:**
- A: Yes; it follows the rules of categorical logic.
- B: No; reptiles and mammals are both vertebrates.
- C: No; some reptiles have high body temperatures.
- D: No; the conclusion restates information already in the premises.
- E: Yes; and the conclusion is also factually true.
**correct_answer:** A
**clue:** Check the logical form, ignoring real-world facts. Does the conclusion follow necessarily from the premises?
**explainer:** A is correct—logically valid (if premises are true, conclusion must be true). E is also technically correct but is less precise (validity and truth are different properties). B, C, D are about factual truth or describe irrelevant issues, not logical validity. This is a valid categorical syllogism.
**difficulty:** Medium

### Item VCR-11
**id:** VCR-11
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "A study of 2,000 healthcare workers found that those who took regular breaks reported 34% fewer errors in patient care. The healthcare ministry recommends implementing mandatory 15-minute breaks every 2 hours based on this finding."
**question:** The recommendation would be most strengthened if which additional information were available?
**options:**
- A: The breaks should include stepping outside for fresh air.
- B: Other healthcare systems have adopted similar break policies.
- C: The study controlled for experience level and shift length.
- D: Different types of errors were weighted equally in the analysis.
- E: The study was conducted during peak and off-peak hours.
**correct_answer:** C
**clue:** The study shows correlation between breaks and fewer errors. What would make the *causal inference* more credible?
**explainer:** C is strongest—controlling for confounders (experience, shift length) strengthens the case that breaks specifically caused the error reduction. B provides precedent (useful but not methodological strength). A specifies break content (reasonable but not validated). D and E are methodological details but less critical than ruling out confounders.
**difficulty:** Hard

### Item VCR-12
**id:** VCR-12
**category:** Verbal Critical Reasoning
**stimulus:** "The new antibiotic kills 99.9% of the target bacteria *in vitro*. Therefore, patients taking the antibiotic will recover quickly from bacterial infections."
**question:** Which of the following best explains why the conclusion **does not follow** from the premise?
**options:**
- A: In vitro effectiveness does not guarantee in vivo effectiveness.
- B: 99.9% effectiveness is not high enough to ensure recovery.
- C: Some bacteria are already resistant to antibiotics.
- D: Recovery speed depends on patient immune health, not just the antibiotic.
- E: The antibiotic may have side effects that slow recovery.

Data Interpretation tests your ability to extract patterns, calculate relationships, and make inferences from tables, charts, and raw data. Format: **Multi-part data set, 1–3 questions per set**.

### Item DIR-01
**id:** DIR-01
**category:** Data Interpretation & Quantitative Reasoning
**stimulus:**
```
TABLE: Regional Sales by Quarter (in £1000s)
Region | Q1  | Q2  | Q3  | Q4
North  | 120 | 140 | 155 | 160
South  | 100 | 110 | 105 | 115
East   | 90  | 100 | 130 | 125
West   | 110 | 115 | 120 | 135

TOTALS: Q1=420, Q2=465, Q3=510, Q4=535
```
**question:** Which region showed the most volatile (inconsistent) growth across the four quarters?
**options:**
- A: North (steady growth)
- B: South (slow, irregular growth)
- C: East (spike, then plateau)
- D: West (steady growth)
- E: All regions grew at the same rate
**correct_answer:** C
**clue:** Calculate the quarter-to-quarter changes for each region. Which has the biggest swings?
**explainer:** C is correct—East Q1→Q2: +10; Q2→Q3: +30 (spike); Q3→Q4: −5 (drop). This is highly volatile. North, South, West show steady, linear patterns. Volatility = magnitude of ups/downs, not just final growth.
**difficulty:** Medium

## Answer-Checking Methodology

### Category 1: Situational Judgement Tests (SJTs)

**Answer Format:** Ranked or rated responses.

**Checking Logic:**
- **Rank items:** Compare candidate's ranking against the answer key (e.g., B, D, A, E, C). Scoring can be:
  - **Exact match (strict):** All 5 items in correct order = pass.
  - **Kendall's tau (lenient):** Measure rank correlation; allow minor inversions of adjacent pairs.
  - **Partial credit:** Award points for correct relative order of high-value items (e.g., best + worst are most important).
- **Rate items:** For 1–5 scales, compare candidate's ratings against the key. Tolerance: ±1 point per item acceptable.

**Passing Threshold:** 70%+ of ranked items in correct order, or average rating within ±1 of the key.

---

### Category 2: Verbal Critical Reasoning

**Answer Format:** Multiple choice (A–E); single correct answer.

**Checking Logic:**
- **Exact match:** Candidate's letter = key letter.
- **No partial credit** (logic is binary: valid or invalid, true or false).
- **Branching (if time permits):** Show the reasoning path if the candidate selects a wrong answer (e.g., "Why did you choose B instead of A?").

**Passing Threshold:** 75%+ correct across the category.

---

### Category 3: Abstract/Diagrammatic/Fluid Reasoning

**Answer Format:** Multiple choice (A–E); single correct answer.

**Checking Logic:**
- **Exact match:** Candidate's letter = key letter. No partial credit.
- **SVG-drawable items:** If a web component renders the patterns visually, compare the candidate's selected option (e.g., "large circle, center-right") against the SVG description of the correct answer.
- **Time measurement (optional):** Track time per item; abstract reasoning is speed-sensitive.

**Passing Threshold:** 70%+ correct. Difficulty-weighted: allow 60% on Hard items if Easy items are ≥85%.

---

### Category 4: Deductive & Analytical Reasoning

**Answer Format:** Multiple choice (A–E); single correct answer.

**Checking Logic:**
- **Exact match:** Candidate's letter = key letter.
- **Logic validation:** For syllogisms, check that the candidate understands valid vs. invalid forms (not just the conclusion). If wrong, highlight which premise/rule was misapplied.
- **No partial credit** (logic is binary).

**Passing Threshold:** 75%+ correct.

---

### Category 5: Data Interpretation & Quantitative Reasoning

**Answer Format:** Multiple choice (A–E); single correct answer, or numeric answers with tolerance.

**Checking Logic:**
- **Multiple choice:** Exact match (letter A–E).
- **Numeric answers (if free-form):** Allow ±5% tolerance (e.g., if correct answer is 1,200, accept 1,140–1,260).
- **Percentage answers:** Accept exact match or ±2 percentage points (e.g., 62% = 60–64% acceptable).
- **Interpretation:** If a candidate's selection has a minor arithmetic error but the methodology is sound, offer feedback (e.g., "Your logic was right; check your calculator").

**Passing Threshold:** 70%+ correct (numeric) or 75%+ correct (conceptual).
