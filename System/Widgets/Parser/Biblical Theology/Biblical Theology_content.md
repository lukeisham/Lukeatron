# Biblical Theology — Content Source

## 1. Foundational Framework: Two-Layer Redemptive-Historical Structure

This content source organises the whole-Bible narrative into two complementary layers. Layer 1 captures the *theological* structure — the four great acts of the redemptive drama. Layer 2 captures the *historical-chronological* structure — the successive epochs through which God unfolds His one plan. Both layers are drawn from the broad Reformed/evangelical tradition of biblical theology (Vos, Goldsworthy, and successors). All Scripture references below are ones the author is highly confident are accurate; any reference flagged `[UNVERIFIED — check before use]` should be validated against a reliable Bible text before this file is compiled.

## 2. Layer 1 — The Four-Act Redemptive Structure

### 2.1 Act 1: Creation

**2.1.1** God creates all things good by His sovereign word (Genesis 1–2). The creation is ordered, purposeful, and declared “very good.”

**2.1.2** Humanity is made in the image of God (imago Dei), male and female, and given the creation mandate to fill, subdue, and rule the earth under God (Genesis 1:26–28).

**2.1.3** God establishes a covenant relationship with humanity in Eden — often termed the covenant of works or covenant of creation in Reformed theology. Adam functions as a covenant head and representative.

**2.1.4** The creation account presents Eden as a temple-garden where God dwells with His people, establishing the temple/presence theme that runs through the whole canon (see §4.3).

**2.1.5** Sabbath rest (Genesis 2:1–3) points forward to the eschatological rest into which God will bring His redeemed people (cf. Hebrews 4:1–11).

### 2.2 Act 2: Fall

**2.2.1** Through the serpent's deception, Adam and Eve disobey God's command (Genesis 3). Sin, guilt, shame, and death enter the created order.

**2.2.2** The curse is pronounced on the serpent, the woman, and the man. The creation itself is subjected to futility (Genesis 3:14–19).

**2.2.3** The protoevangelium — the first gospel promise — is spoken in Genesis 3:15: the seed of the woman will crush the serpent's head. This is the fountainhead of all subsequent redemptive promise.

**2.2.4** The spread and intensification of sin is traced in Genesis 4–11: Cain and Abel (Genesis 4), the genealogy of death (Genesis 5), the flood and Noahic covenant (Genesis 6–9), the table of nations (Genesis 10), and the tower of Babel (Genesis 11). Humanity scatters in rebellion; God preserves a remnant and a promise-line.

**2.2.5** The Noahic covenant (Genesis 8:20–9:17) establishes God's common-grace preservation of the created order as the stage on which redemption will unfold.

### 2.3 Act 3: Redemption

**2.3.1 Abrahamic Covenant.** God calls Abram out of Ur and makes an unconditional covenant with him, promising land, seed (offspring), and blessing to all nations (Genesis 12:1–3; Genesis 15; Genesis 17). This covenant becomes the foundational redemptive administration through which God will reclaim a people for Himself.

**2.3.2 Exodus and Mosaic Covenant.** God redeems Israel from Egypt by His mighty acts of judgment and deliverance (Exodus 1–15). At Sinai He gives the law, establishes the covenant, and provides the tabernacle as the place of His dwelling among the people (Exodus 19–24; Exodus 25–40; Leviticus). The sacrificial system (Leviticus 1–7; Leviticus 16) provides atonement and typological foreshadowing of Christ's final sacrifice.

**2.3.3 Wilderness and Covenant Faithfulness.** Israel's forty-year wilderness journey (Numbers; Deuteronomy) reveals both God's faithfulness in provision and judgment, and the people's persistent rebellion. Deuteronomy restates the covenant on the plains of Moab, urging covenant fidelity before entry into the land.

**2.3.4 Davidic Covenant and Kingdom.** God makes a covenant with David, promising an everlasting throne and kingdom (2 Samuel 7:8–16). The Davidic king becomes the focal point of Israel's hope — a son of David who will rule with righteousness and extend God's reign to the nations. The Psalms and Wisdom literature (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Job, Song of Songs) arise substantially within this monarchical milieu and give inspired voice to the life of covenant faith, worship, and wisdom under God's kingship.

**2.3.5 Prophetic Promise of the New Covenant.** As Israel's monarchy fractures and falls into idolatry and exile, the prophets announce both judgment and future hope. They promise a new covenant in which God will write His law on hearts, forgive sin definitively, and pour out His Spirit (Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:24–28). They foretell a coming Davidic king (Isaiah 9:6–7; Isaiah 11:1–10), a suffering servant who bears sin (Isaiah 52:13–53:12), and a day when the knowledge of YHWH will cover the earth as the waters cover the sea.

**2.3.6 Christ — Incarnation, Life, Death, Resurrection, Ascension.** In the fullness of time, the eternal Son takes on human flesh (John 1:1–18; Galatians 4:4–5). His life recapitulates Israel's story faithfully: tempted yet sinless, proclaiming the kingdom of God, gathering a new-covenant people. His death is the once-for-all atoning sacrifice (the Lamb of God, John 1:29; propitiation, Romans 3:25; the curse-bearer, Galatians 3:13). His bodily resurrection vindicates His identity, defeats death, and inaugurates the new creation (1 Corinthians 15). His ascension enthrones Him at the right hand of the Father as cosmic Lord (Ephesians 1:20–23; Hebrews 1:3).

**2.3.7 Pentecost and the Church Age.** At Pentecost the exalted Christ pours out the promised Holy Spirit, forming the Church as the new-covenant people of God composed of Jew and Gentile (Acts 2). The gospel advances from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth through apostolic witness (Acts 1:8). The Church lives between the “already” of Christ's finished work and the “not yet” of consummation — growing in grace, suffering with Christ, and proclaiming His lordship until He returns.

### 2.4 Act 4: Restoration / New Creation

**2.4.1** Christ will return personally, visibly, and gloriously to judge the living and the dead (Acts 1:11; 2 Timothy 4:1).

**2.4.2** The dead will be raised — those in Christ to eternal life, those outside Christ to condemnation (John 5:28–29; 1 Corinthians 15:51–57; Revelation 20:11–15).

**2.4.3** God will create a new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells (Isaiah 65:17–25; Revelation 21–22). There will be no more death, mourning, crying, or pain (Revelation 21:4).

**2.4.4** In the new creation the dwelling of God will be with humanity; no temple structure is needed because the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple (Revelation 21:22). The creation mandate, the covenant promise, the kingdom hope, and the temple/presence theme all reach their eschatological fulfilment.

## 3. Layer 2 — The Chronological Story of the Bible

### 3.1 Primeval History (Genesis 1–11)
- Creation, Fall, flood, and Babel.
- Establishes the universal context of the biblical drama: one God, one human race, one problem (sin), one promised Redeemer (Genesis 3:15).

### 3.2 Patriarchal Period (Genesis 12–50)
- Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph.
- The Abrahamic covenant dominates: land, seed, blessing.
- The patriarchs' failures display that salvation depends on God's sovereign grace, not human merit.

### 3.3 Exodus and Wilderness (Exodus–Deuteronomy)
- Bondage in Egypt, the call of Moses, the plagues, Passover, the Red Sea deliverance (Exodus 1–15).
- Covenant at Sinai: law, tabernacle, priesthood, sacrifice (Exodus 19–Leviticus).
- Wilderness wanderings: testing, judgment, provision (Numbers; Deuteronomy).
- Deuteronomy as covenant renewal on the edge of the land.

### 3.4 Conquest and Judges (Joshua, Judges, Ruth)
- Conquest under Joshua: partial possession of the land; covenant renewal at Shechem (Joshua 24).
- The Judges cycle: rebellion → oppression → cry for deliverance → God raises a judge → rest → repeat (Judges 2:11–19).
- Ruth provides a redemptive hinge: a Moabite woman brought into the covenant line of David.

### 3.5 United Kingdom (1–2 Samuel; 1 Kings 1–11; Psalms; Wisdom Literature)
- Samuel, Saul, David, Solomon.
- Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7) as the theocratic high point of the Old Testament monarchy.
- The Psalms give inspired prayer, praise, lament, and royal hope.
- The Wisdom corpus (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Job, Song of Songs) reflects on life lived under God in a fallen world.
- Solomon's temple — the zenith and tragic beginning of decline.

### 3.6 Divided Kingdom and Prophets (1 Kings 12–2 Kings; the Prophetic Books)
- The kingdom splits into Israel (north) and Judah (south) after Solomon's death.
- The writing prophets minister in both kingdoms, calling for covenant faithfulness, denouncing idolatry and injustice, and foretelling both judgment (exile) and future restoration through a new covenant, a new Davidic king, and a new exodus.
- Northern kingdom falls to Assyria (722 BC); Judah endures until the Babylonian conquest (586 BC).

### 3.7 Exile (Ezekiel, Daniel, Esther)
- Judah's exile in Babylon: the land, temple, and monarchy are lost. The curses of the covenant (Deuteronomy 28) reach their climactic expression.
- Ezekiel promises a new heart, a new spirit, and a restored temple (Ezekiel 36–37; Ezekiel 40–48).
- Daniel reveals the sovereignty of God over the nations and the coming of an everlasting kingdom through one like a son of man (Daniel 2; Daniel 7).
- Esther shows God's hidden providence preserving His people in a foreign empire.

### 3.8 Return and Restoration (Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi)
- Cyrus's decree (539 BC) permits a remnant to return (Ezra 1).
- The temple is rebuilt (Haggai; Zechariah) and the walls of Jerusalem restored (Nehemiah).
- Covenant renewal under Ezra and Nehemiah; yet the community remains spiritually fragile — the post-exilic prophets expose persistent sin and point forward to a greater, future work of God.
- Malachi closes the Old Testament awaiting the coming of Elijah and the day of the Lord (Malachi 4:5–6).

### 3.9 Intertestamental Period
- The 400-year span between Malachi and John the Baptist.
- Historical developments (not inspired Scripture but critical context): the rise of the Greek empire, the Maccabean revolt, the Hasmonean dynasty, the emergence of Pharisees, Sadducees, and synagogues, and Roman occupation.
- The religious and political landscape of first-century Judea is the stage prepared for the Messiah's arrival.
- Reference: the Apocryphal/Deuterocanonical books (especially 1 & 2 Maccabees) provide historical narrative for portions of this period.

### 3.10 Gospels and the Life of Christ (Matthew–John)
- The incarnation, birth, and early life of Jesus; John the Baptist's ministry.
- Jesus' public ministry: teaching, miracles, parables, and the proclamation of the kingdom of God.
- His movement toward Jerusalem, passion week, Last Supper, trial, crucifixion, burial.
- His bodily resurrection and post-resurrection appearances.
- The Great Commission (Matthew 28:18–20).
- Each Gospel presents Christ from a complementary angle (Matthew: Messiah-King; Mark: Suffering Servant; Luke: Son of Man for all peoples; John: eternal Son of God).

### 3.11 Acts and the Early Church
- Ascension of Christ; Pentecost and the gift of the Spirit (Acts 1–2).
- The gospel's expansion: Jerusalem (Acts 1–7), Judea and Samaria (Acts 8), to the Gentiles (Acts 10–11), and to the ends of the earth through Paul's missionary journeys (Acts 13–28).
- The Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) affirms justification by grace through faith for Jew and Gentile alike.
- The book closes with Paul in Rome, the gospel unhindered (Acts 28:30–31).

### 3.12 Epistles and Church Teaching
- Paul's letters (Romans–Philemon): exposition of the gospel, pastoral instruction, ecclesiology, and eschatology. Justification by faith alone, union with Christ, sanctification by the Spirit, and the mystery of Jew-Gentile unity in one body.
- The General/Catholic Epistles (Hebrews–Jude): perseverance in faith, the supremacy of Christ's priesthood (Hebrews), wisdom in trials (James), suffering as a Christian (1 Peter), discernment against false teaching (2 Peter; Jude), and the assurance of fellowship with God (1–3 John).
- The Epistles apply the once-for-all work of Christ to the life of local churches and individual believers, sustaining them in the period between Christ's ascension and return.

### 3.13 Apocalyptic and Consummation (Revelation)
- John's vision of the risen and reigning Christ (Revelation 1).
- Letters to the seven churches: encouragement and warning for the Church in every age (Revelation 2–3).
- Visions of judgment, the Lamb's victory, the fall of Babylon, and the final defeat of Satan (Revelation 4–20).
- The new heaven, new earth, and new Jerusalem (Revelation 21–22).
- The book closes with the Church's cry “Come, Lord Jesus!” (Revelation 22:20) and the benediction of grace.

## 4. Key Biblical-Theological Themes Traced Through the Canon

### 4.1 Covenant

**4.1.1** Covenant is the central structuring device of God's relationship with humanity in Scripture. It provides the legal-relational framework within which redemption unfolds.

**4.1.2** Major divine covenants:
- **Creation covenant (Adamic).** Implied in the creation mandate and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. God as sovereign Lord, Adam as covenant servant-representative. Reformed theology terms this the covenant of works.
- **Noahic covenant (Genesis 8:20–9:17).** Universal in scope; God promises to uphold the created order until redemption is complete. The sign is the rainbow.
- **Abrahamic covenant (Genesis 12:1–3; Genesis 15; Genesis 17).** The redemptive covenant par excellence: promise of land, seed, and universal blessing. The sign is circumcision. This covenant is unilateral, gracious, and everlasting.
- **Mosaic covenant (Exodus 19–24).** Given at Sinai; law, priesthood, and sacrifice regulate Israel's life as God's holy nation. Conditional blessings and curses (Deuteronomy 28) define its administration while resting upon the prior Abrahamic promises.
- **Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7:8–16; Psalm 89).** The promise of an everlasting throne and a son who will build God's house. David's dynasty becomes the channel of messianic hope.
- **New covenant (Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:24–28; Luke 22:20; Hebrews 8–10).** Inaugurated by Christ's blood, internalised by the Spirit, and characterised by the full forgiveness of sins and the knowledge of God.

**4.1.3** The new covenant is not an abandonment of earlier covenants but their fulfilment. Christ is the true seed of Abraham (Galatians 3:16), the true Israel, the Son of David, and the mediator of a better covenant.

### 4.2 Kingdom

**4.2.1** God is the eternal King over all creation (Psalm 93; Psalm 97; Psalm 99). Human kingship is always derivative and accountable to Him.

**4.2.2** The kingdom theme develops through:
- **Eden:** God reigns directly; Adam serves as vicegerent.
- **Theocracy in Israel:** God as Israel's true King; the judges and later the monarchy as delegates. Samuel's warning about kingship (1 Samuel 8) exposes the tension between human kingship and divine rule.
- **Davidic kingship:** The Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7) establishes the king as God's adopted son who will rule in righteousness. Messianic psalms (Psalm 2; Psalm 110) invest the king with worldwide dominion.
- **Prophetic kingdom hope:** The prophets anticipate a coming Davidic king whose reign will bring justice, peace, and the knowledge of God to the nations (Isaiah 11:1–10; Daniel 7:13–14).
- **Christ's kingdom:** Jesus inaugurates the kingdom in His person and work (Mark 1:15). His kingdom is already present — in His miracles, exorcisms, and teaching — yet awaits its consummation at His return. The Church is the community of the kingdom, living under Christ's lordship.
- **Consummation:** At Christ's return, He will deliver the kingdom to the Father (1 Corinthians 15:24–28). The new creation will be a kingdom of priests reigning with God forever (Revelation 22:5).

### 4.3 Temple / Presence of God

**4.3.1** The temple theme traces God's desire to dwell with His people:
- **Eden as proto-temple:** God walks with Adam and Eve in the garden (Genesis 3:8); the garden is a sanctuary where heaven and earth meet. The cherubim guarding its entrance after the Fall (Genesis 3:24) parallel the cherubim in the tabernacle and temple.
- **Tabernacle:** After the Exodus, God instructs Moses to build a portable sanctuary — “let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell in their midst” (Exodus 25:8). The tabernacle's design mirrors heavenly reality and mediates God's holy presence.
- **Solomon's temple:** The permanent structure on Mount Zion becomes the centre of Israel's worship and the visible sign of God's dwelling among His covenant people (1 Kings 8:10–11).
- **Destruction and exile:** The departure of the glory of God from the temple (Ezekiel 10) and the temple's destruction (586 BC) represent the catastrophic loss of God's presence — the ultimate covenant curse.
- **Second temple and eschatological hope:** The rebuilt temple (Ezra 3–6; Haggai) lacks the glory of Solomon's; yet the prophets promise a greater eschatological temple (Ezekiel 40–48) to which the nations will stream.
- **Christ as the true temple:** “The Word became flesh and tabernacled among us” (John 1:14). Jesus' body is the true meeting place between God and humanity. He is greater than the temple (Matthew 12:6). His death opens access to God (the tearing of the temple veil, Matthew 27:51).
- **The Church as the temple:** Believers, indwelt by the Spirit, are corporately the temple of the living God (1 Corinthians 3:16–17; Ephesians 2:19–22; 1 Peter 2:4–5).
- **New creation as temple:** In the new Jerusalem there is no temple building — “for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb” (Revelation 21:22). The entire new creation becomes the dwelling place of God with redeemed humanity.

### 4.4 Sacrifice / Atonement

**4.4.1** The shedding of blood for the remission of sins is woven through the whole biblical narrative:
- The first sacrifice occurs when God clothes Adam and Eve with skins (Genesis 3:21) — an implicit substitutionary death.
- The Passover lamb (Exodus 12) delivers Israel from judgment through the blood applied to the doorposts.
- The Levitical system (Leviticus 1–7) provides a typological grammar of atonement: burnt offerings, sin offerings, guilt offerings, and especially the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16) with its two goats — one slain, one sent away bearing sin.
- The prophets connect sacrifice to a suffering person: Isaiah 52:13–53:12 portrays the Servant of the Lord as a guilt offering who bears the sin of many.
- The New Testament identifies Christ's death as the fulfilment of all sacrifices — He is the Passover lamb (1 Corinthians 5:7), the propitiation for sins (Romans 3:25; 1 John 2:2), the sin-bearer (2 Corinthians 5:21), the ransom for many (Mark 10:45), and the great high priest who offers Himself (Hebrews 9:11–14; Hebrews 10:1–18).
- Christ's once-for-all sacrifice (Hebrews 10:10–14) renders the sacrificial system obsolete, having accomplished what the blood of bulls and goats could never do.

### 4.5 Exile / Return

**4.5.1** Exile is the great Old Testament crisis — the loss of land, king, and temple — standing under the covenant curses (Deuteronomy 28:64–68; Leviticus 26:33–39).
- The Northern Kingdom falls to Assyria (722 BC; 2 Kings 17).
- Judah falls to Babylon in stages culminating in the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple (586 BC; 2 Kings 25).

**4.5.2** Exile in the prophets becomes a theological paradigm for the human condition — separation from God's presence because of sin.

**4.5.3** Return from exile is promised as a new exodus: God will gather His scattered people, cleanse them, put His Spirit within them, and bring them back to the land (Isaiah 40; Jeremiah 31; Ezekiel 36–37).

**4.5.4** The post-exilic return (Ezra–Nehemiah) is partial and anticlimactic; the prophets make clear that the greater, eschatological return remains future.

**4.5.5** Jesus' ministry enacts the true return from exile: He forgives sins, heals the broken, gathers outcasts, and reconstitutes Israel around Himself. His death and resurrection accomplish the decisive return — breaking the power of sin, death, and separation from God.

**4.5.6** The Church lives as the community of the returned-from-exile, though still pilgrims (1 Peter 2:11) awaiting the final homecoming in the new creation.

### 4.6 Promise / Fulfilment

**4.6.1** The Bible's unity rests on the dynamic of divine promise and historical fulfilment. Promises made in the Old Testament find their “yes” and “amen” in Christ (2 Corinthians 1:20).

**4.6.2** Key promise trajectories:
- **Seed of the woman (Genesis 3:15)** → Christ's victory over Satan (Romans 16:20; Revelation 12).
- **Abrahamic seed and blessing (Genesis 12:1–3)** → Christ as the true seed and the gospel going to all nations (Galatians 3:8, 16).
- **Passover lamb (Exodus 12)** → Christ as the Lamb of God (John 1:29; 1 Corinthians 5:7).
- **Davidic throne (2 Samuel 7)** → Christ's eternal reign (Luke 1:32–33; Acts 2:30–36).
- **New covenant (Jeremiah 31)** → Inaugurated in Christ's blood (Luke 22:20; Hebrews 8).
- **Suffering Servant (Isaiah 53)** → Christ's atoning death (Acts 8:32–35; 1 Peter 2:24).
- **New heavens and new earth (Isaiah 65:17)** → The new Jerusalem (Revelation 21:1–5).

**4.6.3** The hermeneutical principle: reading the Bible with Christ and His gospel as the centre of gravity (Luke 24:27, 44). The Old Testament is not merely a collection of moral examples or predictive proof-texts; it is the unfolding story that finds its coherence and climax in the person and work of Jesus Christ.

## 5. Hermeneutical Commitments (for the Parser Aide)

**5.1** The Bible is a unified canon whose divine Author progressively discloses His one redemptive plan across the historical unfolding of the Testaments.

**5.2** Scripture interprets Scripture: unclear passages are understood in light of clearer ones; the whole canon illuminates each part.

**5.3** The New Testament's apostolic interpretation of the Old Testament is normative and authoritative for understanding how the Old Testament bears witness to Christ.

**5.4** Typology — the study of persons, events, and institutions divinely designed to prefigure their eschatological fulfilment in Christ — is a legitimate and essential biblical-theological method. Types are discernible through the progressive repetition and escalation of patterns across redemptive history.

**5.5** The redemptive-historical approach prioritises the *historia salutis* (the unfolding history of redemption) as the primary context for understanding any text, while affirming that every text also addresses the *ordo salutis* (the application of redemption to individuals) in ways consistent with its place in that history.

**5.6** The law-gospel distinction (a hallmark of Reformed biblical theology): the law commands and condemns, revealing sin and driving sinners to Christ; the gospel announces what God has done in Christ, granting righteousness and life freely through faith.

**5.7** Eschatology is not confined to the end times but is a dimension of the whole Bible: the already–not yet tension characterises the present age between Christ's first and second comings.
