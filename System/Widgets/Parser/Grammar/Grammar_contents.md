# Grammar Content 

1. *Sentential Level*
   The highest level of grammatical organisation. A sentence expresses a complete, independent thought and may contain one or more clauses.
1.1 Simple Sentence
   Contains exactly one independent clause.
   Example: "Call me Ishmael." (Herman Melville, *Moby-Dick*) / "You have brains in your head." (Dr. Seuss, *Oh, the Places You'll Go!*)
1.2 Compound Sentence
   Contains two or more independent clauses joined by coordination (e.g., and, but, or) or punctuation (semicolon, dash).
   Example: "It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen." (George Orwell, *1984*) / "Two roads diverged in a wood, and I— / I took the one less traveled by," (Robert Frost, *The Road Not Taken*)
1.3 Complex Sentence
   Contains one independent clause and at least one dependent (subordinate) clause.
   Example: "The boy stood on the burning deck, whence all but him had fled." (Felicia Hemans, *Casabianca*)
1.4 Compound-Complex Sentence
   Contains at least two independent clauses and one or more dependent clauses.
   Example: "He was an old man who fished alone in a skiff in the Gulf Stream and he had gone eighty-four days now without taking a fish." (Ernest Hemingway, *The Old Man and the Sea*)
1.5 Sentences Joined Primarily by Punctuation
   Independent clauses joined by semicolons, colons, or dashes without coordinating conjunctions.
   Example: "To be, or not to be: that is the question." (William Shakespeare, *Hamlet*)
1.6 Sentences with Mixed Coordination and Subordination
   Combines coordination, subordination, and punctuation for more elaborate structures.
   Example: "I meant what I said and I said what I meant." (Dr. Seuss, *Horton Hatches the Egg*)

1.7 Sentences Classified by Purpose
   Sentences can also be classified according to their communicative purpose.
   - **Declarative Sentence**: Makes a statement or expresses an opinion.
     Example: "Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal." (Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg Address)
   - **Interrogative Sentence**: Asks a direct question.
     Example: "What light through yonder window breaks?" (William Shakespeare, *Romeo and Juliet*)
   - **Imperative Sentence**: Gives a command, request, or instruction. (See also 5.7.6)
     Example: "Do not go gentle into that good night." (Dylan Thomas, *Do not go gentle into that good night*)
   - **Exclamatory Sentence**: Expresses strong emotion, surprise, or emphasis.
     Example: "What a piece of work is a man! how noble in reason! how infinite in faculty!" (William Shakespeare, *Hamlet*)
   - **Tag Question**: A declarative sentence with a short interrogative "tag" appended, inviting confirmation. The tag reverses the polarity of the main clause (positive statement → negative tag, and vice versa) and repeats its auxiliary and subject pronoun.
     Example: "It's a nice day, isn't it?" / "You haven't forgotten, have you?"

2. *Clausal Level*
   Clauses form the core building blocks of sentences. A clause is a structure that contains a subject and a predicate.
2.1 Independent Clause (Main Clause)
   Able to stand alone as a complete sentence.
   Example: "Call me Ishmael." (Herman Melville, *Moby-Dick*)
2.2 Dependent Clause (Subordinate Clause)
   Cannot stand alone and must attach to an independent clause. Often introduced by subordinating conjunctions (because, although, when, if, etc.).
   Example: "as long as it stays inside the maze." (Margaret Atwood, *The Handmaid's Tale*)
2.3 Noun Clause (Nominal Clause)
   A dependent clause that functions as a noun — acting as a subject, object, or complement. Often introduced by "that," "what," "whoever," etc. An expletive "it" (dummy subject) can delay the true noun clause to the end of the sentence.
   Example: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." (Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice*)
Core Definition: Every clause contains a subject + predicate.
Example: "Call me Ishmael" (subject: implied "you"; predicate: "Call me Ishmael"). (Herman Melville, *Moby-Dick*)

2.4 Adverbial Clause
   A dependent clause that functions as an adverb, modifying a verb, adjective, or another adverb in the main clause. It typically expresses time, place, reason, condition, concession, manner, or purpose.
   Example: "If music be the food of love, play on." (William Shakespeare, *Twelfth Night*)

2.5 Relative Clause (Adjectival Clause)
   A dependent clause that functions as an adjective, modifying a noun or pronoun. It is introduced by a relative pronoun (who, whom, whose, which, that) or relative adverb (where, when, why).
   Example: "the Grinch, who lived just north of Who-ville, did NOT!" (Dr. Seuss, *How the Grinch Stole Christmas!*)

2.6 Clause Elements (Functional Roles)
   Within a clause, phrases fill a small set of functional roles. Every clause has a subject and a verb; the remaining elements depend on the verb's type.
   - **Subject (S)**: The doer or topic the clause is about.
     Example: "The cat ran at the rat." (McGuffey, *Eclectic Primer* — "The cat" is the subject)
   - **Verb (V)**: The action, state, or occurrence, with any auxiliaries.
   - **Direct Object (dO)**: The entity directly acted upon by a transitive verb.
     Example: "I shot the sheriff." (Bob Marley, *I Shot the Sheriff* — "the sheriff" is the direct object)
   - **Indirect Object (iO)**: The recipient or beneficiary, appearing before the direct object (or paraphrasable with "to"/"for").
     Example: "I gave my love a cherry." (traditional folk song — "my love" is the indirect object; "a cherry" the direct object)
   - **Subject Complement (sC)**: Follows a linking verb (be, seem, become) and renames or describes the subject.
     Example: "I am the walrus." (The Beatles, *I Am the Walrus* — "the walrus" complements "I")
   - **Object Complement (oC)**: Follows and completes a direct object, renaming or describing it.
     Example: "…they would come and take him by force, to make him a king…" (John 6:15, KJV — "a king" complements "him")
   - **Adverbial (A)**: An optional element of time, place, manner, reason, etc. — an adverb phrase, prepositional phrase, or noun phrase acting adverbially.
     Example: "Toto, I've a feeling we're not in Kansas anymore." (*The Wizard of Oz* — "in Kansas" is an adverbial of place; "anymore" of time)
   Common patterns: S-V ("He slept."), S-V-dO, S-V-iO-dO, S-V-sC, S-V-dO-oC — each optionally extended by adverbials.

3. *Phrasal Level*
   Groups of words that function together as a single syntactic unit within a clause. Subjects and objects are typically noun phrases.
3.1 Noun Phrase (NP)
   A group of words centred on a noun (can include determiners, adjectives, etc.).
   Example: "the big red ball"
3.2 Verb Phrase (VP)
   A group of words centred on a verb (may include auxiliaries, adverbs, etc.).
   Example: "has been running quickly"
3.3 Prepositional Phrase
   Preposition + its object (usually a noun phrase).
   Example: "From there to here, from here to there, funny things are everywhere." (Dr. Seuss, *One Fish Two Fish Red Fish Blue Fish*)
3.4 Adjective Phrase
   Group of words that modifies a noun.
   Example: "truer than true" (Dr. Seuss, *Happy Birthday to You!*)
3.5 Adverb Phrase
   Group of words that modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb.
   Example: "extremely quickly"
3.6 Participial Phrase
   A phrase headed by a participle (present -ing or past -ed/-en form) that functions as an adjective.
   Example: "…he'd reach out to touch the child sleeping beside him." (Cormac McCarthy, *The Road*) / "…breeding / Lilacs out of the dead land…" (T.S. Eliot, *The Waste Land*)
3.7 Infinitive Phrase
   A phrase headed by "to" + the base form of a verb. It can function as a noun, adjective, or adverb.
   Example: "To be, or not to be: that is the question." (William Shakespeare, *Hamlet*)
3.8 Gerund Phrase
   A phrase headed by a gerund (-ing form of a verb) that functions as a noun.
   Example: "Swimming in the ocean is refreshing." / "She enjoys reading mystery novels."
3.9 Absolute Phrase (Nominative Absolute)
   A phrase that modifies an entire independent clause rather than a single word. It consists of a noun + modifier (often a participle) but lacks a finite verb, carrying its own internal subject distinct from the main clause.
   Example: "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past." (F. Scott Fitzgerald, *The Great Gatsby*)

3.10 Appositive Phrase
   A noun phrase that immediately follows and renames or explains another noun or pronoun. It may be essential (restrictive, no commas) or non-essential (non-restrictive, set off by commas).
   Example: "Atticus Finch, a quiet and dignified lawyer in Maycomb, defended an innocent man." (Harper Lee, *To Kill a Mockingbird*)

4. *Lexical / Morphological Level*

4.1 Word Classes (Parts of Speech)
    **Nouns** (common, proper, countable, uncountable, collective, abstract)  
      Words that name people, places, things, or ideas.
    **Verbs** (main, auxiliary, modal; transitive, intransitive; finite, non-finite)  
      Words that express actions, states, or occurrences.
    **Adjectives**  
      Words that describe or modify nouns.
    **Adverbs**  
      Words that modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs.
    **Pronouns** (personal, possessive, reflexive, relative, demonstrative, indefinite, interrogative, reciprocal)  
      Words that replace nouns. Interrogative pronouns (who, whom, whose, which, what) introduce direct questions — "What light through yonder window breaks?" (Shakespeare, *Romeo and Juliet*). Reciprocal pronouns (each other, one another) express mutual action.
    **Prepositions**  
      Words that show relationships between nouns/pronouns and other words.
    **Conjunctions** (coordinating, subordinating, correlative)  
      Words that join words, phrases, or clauses.
    **Determiners / Articles**  
      Words that introduce and specify nouns (a, an, the, this, some, etc.).
    **Interjections**  
      Words expressing sudden emotion or exclamation.

4.2 Morphological Features
    **Inflection** (plurals, tenses, degrees of comparison)  
      Changes in word form to express grammatical features such as number, tense, or degree.
    **Derivation** (prefixes, suffixes, compounding)  
      Creating new words by adding affixes or combining existing words.

4.3 Verb Tenses and Aspects
   English verbs combine **tense** (present, past, future) with **aspect** to express time, duration, completion, and ongoing action. There are 12 core active forms. These interact with voice (see 5.5.1), modality (see 5.5.2), and the subjunctive (see 5.7.1).

   **Formation overview**:
   - Simple: base form or -ed (past)
   - Progressive (continuous): be + -ing
   - Perfect: have + past participle
   - Perfect progressive: have been + -ing

   4.3.1 Present Simple  
      Form: base form (3rd person singular adds -s/-es)  
      Uses: habits, facts, scheduled future, timeless truths.  
      Example: "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times…" (Charles Dickens, *A Tale of Two Cities*) — narrative use of past simple for vividness, but present simple is equally common in timeless statements.

   4.3.2 Present Progressive (Continuous)  
      Form: am/is/are + -ing  
      Uses: actions happening now, temporary situations, changing/developing situations.  
      Example: "I am the walrus." (The Beatles – often cited in grammar discussions for its progressive form)

   4.3.3 Present Perfect  
      Form: have/has + past participle  
      Uses: actions with present relevance, life experience, unfinished time periods.  
      Example: "I have been here before." (common literary motif; e.g., variations appear in many novels)

   4.3.4 Present Perfect Progressive  
      Form: have/has been + -ing  
      Uses: actions that started in the past and continue, or have recently stopped with visible results.  
      Example: "I have been working on this for years." (frequent in reflective narration)

   4.3.5 Past Simple  
      Form: -ed (irregular forms vary)  
      Uses: completed actions at a specific past time, sequences of events.  
      Example: "Call me Ishmael." (Herman Melville, *Moby-Dick*) — though imperative, past simple dominates narrative.

   4.3.6 Past Progressive  
      Form: was/were + -ing  
      Uses: ongoing actions in the past, background actions, interrupted actions.  
      Example: "He was an old man who fished alone in a skiff…" (Ernest Hemingway, *The Old Man and the Sea*)

   4.3.7 Past Perfect  
      Form: had + past participle  
      Uses: actions completed before another past action (backshifting).  
      Example: "She had gone eighty-four days now without taking a fish." (Ernest Hemingway, *The Old Man and the Sea*)

   4.3.8 Past Perfect Progressive  
      Form: had been + -ing  
      Uses: ongoing actions before another past point.  
      Example: Used frequently in complex narratives for duration before a past event.

   4.3.9 Future Simple (will / be going to)  
      Form: will + base form or am/is/are going to + base form  
      Uses: predictions, spontaneous decisions, planned future.  
      Example: "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow…" (William Shakespeare, *Macbeth*) — future reference

   4.3.10 Future Progressive  
      Form: will be + -ing  
      Uses: actions in progress at a future time.  
      Example: Common in speculative or descriptive future scenes in literature.

   4.3.11 Future Perfect  
      Form: will have + past participle  
      Uses: actions completed before a future point.  
      Example: "By the time you read this, I will have left." (common epistolary style)

   4.3.12 Future Perfect Progressive  
      Form: will have been + -ing  
      Uses: duration up to a future point.  
      Example: Rare but used for emphasis on ongoing future duration.

   **Interactions**:
   - With passive voice: "The book has been read." (present perfect passive)
   - With modals: "She might have been working." (modal + perfect progressive)
   - With subjunctive: "If I were going…" (subjunctive + progressive)

4.4 Determiners and Quantifiers
   Determiners are words that introduce nouns and give information about definiteness, quantity, possession, or specificity. They always come before adjectives in a noun phrase.

   4.4.1 Articles
      - **Indefinite articles** (a/an): non-specific singular countable nouns.
        Example: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." (Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice*)
      - **Definite article** (the): specific or known nouns.
        Example: "The boy stood on the burning deck…" (Felicia Hemans, *Casabianca*)
      - **Zero article**: plural or uncountable nouns in general statements.
        Example: "Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink." (Samuel Taylor Coleridge, *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*)

   4.4.2 Demonstratives
      this / that (singular) — these / those (plural)  
      Indicate proximity or distance (physical or figurative).  
      Example: "This above all: to thine own self be true." (William Shakespeare, *Hamlet*)

   4.4.3 Possessive Determiners
      my, your, his, her, its, our, their  
      Show ownership or relationship.  
      Example: "My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains / My sense…" (John Keats, *Ode to a Nightingale*)

   4.4.4 Quantifiers
      Express amount or number. Common categories:
      - **Large quantities**: much, many, a lot of, plenty of, a great deal of
      - **Small quantities**: little, few, a little, a few
      - **All / none / some**: all, both, either, neither, some, any, no
      - **Individual / distributive**: each, every, either, neither
      Example: "A person's a person, no matter how small." (Dr. Seuss, *Horton Hears a Who!*)
      Example: "All the world's a stage…" (William Shakespeare, *As You Like It*)

   4.4.5 Numerals
      Cardinal numerals (one, two, eighty-four) and ordinal numerals (first, second) are classified as **quantifiers/determiners**, not adjectives, despite their attributive position. Distributional evidence: they must precede any true adjective ("eighty-four *long* days", never "*long eighty-four days*"), cannot be graded ("*very eighty-four*"), and cannot be predicative in the adjectival sense. Traditional school grammar calls attributive numbers adjectives; this taxonomy follows the modern determiner/quantifier classification.
      Example: "Four score and seven years ago…" (Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg Address) / "…he had gone eighty-four days now without taking a fish." (Ernest Hemingway, *The Old Man and the Sea*)

   **Key rules**:
   - Articles and most determiners cannot be used together ("the my book" is incorrect).
   - Some quantifiers are used only with countable nouns (many, few, several), others only with uncountable (much, little), and some with both (some, any, all).
   - "A few" vs "few" and "a little" vs "little" carry different meanings (positive vs negative implication).

4.5 Particles and Phrasal Verbs
   A particle is a small word (up, down, off, out, on) that combines with a verb to form a **phrasal verb** whose meaning is not predictable from its parts ("give up" ≠ give + up). Particles look like prepositions or adverbs but belong to the verb: unlike a preposition, a particle can move past a noun object ("give the fight up") and *must* follow a pronoun object ("give it up", never "*give up it").
   Example: "Never gonna give you up, never gonna let you down." (Rick Astley, *Never Gonna Give You Up* — two separable phrasal verbs with the pronoun object between verb and particle)
   Primarily affects: Lexical & Phrasal Levels

5. Governing Conventions

5.1 Agreement Rules
These ensure consistency between related elements.
5.1.1 Subject-Verb Agreement
   The subject and verb must agree in number and person.
   Example: "The dog barks" (singular) vs. "The dogs bark" (plural).
   Primarily affects: Clausal Level
5.1.2 Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement
   Pronouns must agree with their antecedents in number, gender, and person.
   Example: "The students submitted their assignments."
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels

5.2 Order Rules
These govern the conventional sequence of elements.
5.2.1 Adjectival Order (Royal Order of Adjectives)
   Opinion → Size → Age → Shape → Colour → Origin → Material → Purpose
   Example: "a lovely small old round red Italian leather handbag"
   Primarily affects: Phrasal Level (Noun Phrases)
5.2.2 Adverbial Order
   Manner → Place → Frequency → Time → Purpose
   Example: "She sang beautifully in the hall every evening last week for charity."
   Primarily affects: Phrasal & Clausal Levels

5.3 Structural & Omission Rules
5.3.1 Parallelism (Parallel Structure)
   Elements in a list, comparison, or coordinated structure must have the same grammatical form.
   Example: "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness…" (Charles Dickens, *A Tale of Two Cities*)
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels
5.3.2 Implied / Elliptical Subjects
   The subject may be omitted when it is clear from context (common in compound sentences and imperatives).
   Example: "The dog barked and [it] ran away." / "Call me Ishmael." (implied "you" — Herman Melville, *Moby-Dick*)
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels
5.3.3 Zero That (Omitted Subordinating Conjunction)
   In English, the subordinating conjunction "that" may be omitted when introducing a nominal dependent clause, leaving the clause to follow the main verb directly.
   Example: "Last night I dreamt I went to Manderley again." (Daphne du Maurier, *Rebecca*)
   Primarily affects: Clausal Level
5.3.4 Existential "There" (Dummy Subjects)
   "There" may occupy the subject position as a dummy (expletive) subject, asserting existence; the true (notional) subject follows the verb and controls verb agreement. Companion to the expletive "it" (see 2.3).
   Example: "There was no possibility of taking a walk that day." (Charlotte Brontë, *Jane Eyre*)
   Primarily affects: Clausal Level

5.4 Consistency Rules
5.4.1 Tense Consistency
   Maintain logical tense progression within a sentence or paragraph unless a deliberate shift is signalled.
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels
5.4.2 Article / Determiner & Quantifier Usage
   Correct choice and placement of articles (a, an, the) and quantifiers.
   Example: "She saw an elephant at the zoo." (not "a elephant")
   Primarily affects: Phrasal & Clausal Levels

5.5 Voice & Modality
5.5.1 Voice (Active vs Passive)
   Active — subject performs the action. Passive — subject receives the action.
   Form (passive): be + past participle (+ by + agent).
   Example: Active — "The cat ran at the rat." (McGuffey, *Eclectic Primer*) Passive — "The cat was chased by the dog."
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels
5.5.2 Modality (Modal Verbs)
   Express necessity, possibility, permission, or ability (can, could, may, might, must, should, etc.).
   Example: "…you have to know how." (Dr. Seuss, *The Cat in the Hat*) / "She might come tomorrow."
   Primarily affects: Clausal Level

5.6 Negation Rules
5.6.1 Basic Negation
   Use of not, n't, never, no, etc. to reverse meaning. Negating a main verb requires **do-support**: an auxiliary "do" carries the tense so "not" can attach ("I do not like…", never "*I like not…"); verbs that are already auxiliary or modal negate directly ("is not", "cannot").
   Example: "I do not like green eggs and ham." (Dr. Seuss, *Green Eggs and Ham*) / "A person's a person, no matter how small." (Dr. Seuss, *Horton Hears a Who!*) / "Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink." (Samuel Taylor Coleridge, *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*)
   Primarily affects: Clausal Level
5.6.2 Double Negation
   Avoid in standard English as it creates a positive (except in specific dialects).
   Example: "I don't have nothing." (non-standard)

5.7 Advanced Constructions
5.7.1 Subjunctive Mood
   Special verb forms used for hypothetical, wished, or suggested situations.
   Example: "…as if she were the sun, yet he saw her, like the sun, even without looking." (Leo Tolstoy, *Anna Karenina*)
   Primarily affects: Clausal Level
5.7.2 Cleft Sentences
   Sentences that split information to emphasise one part using "it is/was" or "what".
   Example: "It was the dog that barked loudly." / "What she needs is rest."
   Primarily affects: Sentential Level
5.7.3 Relative Clauses (Advanced)
   Non-restrictive (extra information, commas) vs Restrictive (essential, no commas).
   Example: Restrictive — "He was an old man who fished alone in a skiff in the Gulf Stream…" (Ernest Hemingway, *The Old Man and the Sea*) Non-restrictive — "…the Grinch, who lived just north of Who-ville, did NOT!" (Dr. Seuss, *How the Grinch Stole Christmas!*)
5.7.4 Conditional Sentences (Advanced)
   Mixed and inverted conditionals for hypothetical past/present/future scenarios.
   Example: "Unless someone like you cares a whole awful lot, nothing is going to get better." (Dr. Seuss, *The Lorax*)
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels
5.7.5 Inversion
   Reversal of normal subject-verb order for emphasis, questions (subject-auxiliary inversion), or after negative adverbs.
   Example: "How do I love thee? Let me count the ways." (Elizabeth Barrett Browning, *Sonnet 43*) / "Never have I seen such a beautiful sunset."
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels
5.7.6 Imperative Mood
   A verb mood used for commands, requests, or instructions. The subject is typically the implied "you." The imperative can also feature flat adverbs — adverbs that take the same form as their corresponding adjective (e.g., "gentle" rather than "gently").
   Example: "Do not go gentle into that good night." (Dylan Thomas, *Do not go gentle into that good night*)
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels

5.7.7 Direct and Indirect (Reported) Speech
   Direct speech quotes the speaker's exact words inside quotation marks. Indirect (reported) speech embeds them as a subordinate clause without quotation marks, typically **backshifting** the tense (present → past, past → past perfect; see 4.3.7) and shifting pronouns and time/place words (I → he, now → then, here → there).
   Example: Direct — "Frankly, my dear, I don't give a damn." (*Gone with the Wind*) Indirect — Rhett said that, frankly, he didn't give a damn.
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels

5.8 Stylistic Coordination
5.8.1 Polysyndeton
   The deliberate use of multiple coordinating conjunctions in close succession, often with no commas, to create rhythm or a sense of overwhelming magnitude.
   Example: "Think left and think right and think low and think high." (Dr. Seuss, *Oh, the Thinks You Can Think!*)
   Primarily affects: Sentential Level

6. Punctuation & Orthography

6.1 Basic Punctuation Rules
   Use of periods, commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, and quotation marks to clarify meaning and structure.
   Primarily affects: Sentential Level

   - **Period (.)**: Ends a declarative sentence.
     Example: "Call me Ishmael." (Herman Melville, *Moby-Dick*)
   - **Question Mark (?)**: Ends a direct question.
     Example: "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" (William Shakespeare, *Sonnet 18*)
   - **Exclamation Mark (!)**: Indicates strong emotion or emphasis.
     Example: "What a beautiful sunset!"
   - **Semicolon (;)**: Joins closely related independent clauses.
     Example: "Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;" (W.B. Yeats, *The Second Coming*)
   - **Colon (:)**: Introduces lists, explanations, or quotations.
     Example: "To be, or not to be: that is the question." (William Shakespeare, *Hamlet*)
   - **Em Dash (—)**: Indicates a break in thought, interruption, or additional information (stronger than commas or parentheses).
     Example: "Because I could not stop for Death – / He kindly stopped for me –" (Emily Dickinson, *Because I could not stop for Death*)
   - **En Dash (–)**: Used for ranges or connections.
     Example: "Pages 10–15" or "New York–London flight".
   - **Quotation Marks (" ")**: Enclose direct speech or quoted material.
     Example: She said, "The dog is barking."
   - **Hyphen (-)**: Joins the words of a compound modifier before a noun ("a well-known author"), compound numbers (eighty-four), and certain prefixes. Distinct from the en and em dashes above.
     Example: "…he had gone eighty-four days now without taking a fish." (Ernest Hemingway, *The Old Man and the Sea*)
   - **Parentheses ( )**: Enclose supplementary information more detached than commas or dashes would signal; the sentence must read complete without them.
     Example: "My very photogenic mother died in a freak accident (picnic, lightning) when I was three." (Vladimir Nabokov, *Lolita*)
   - **Ellipsis (…)**: Marks omitted words in a quotation, or a trailing-off in dialogue.
     Example: "So we beat on, boats against the current…" (F. Scott Fitzgerald, *The Great Gatsby* — quotation shortened with an ellipsis)

6.2 Comma Usage
   Lists, introductory phrases, non-restrictive clauses, compound sentences, and direct address.
   Example: "She bought apples, oranges, and bananas."

   - **Lists**: Separates items in a series.
     Example: "She bought apples, oranges, and bananas."
   - **Introductory Phrases**: After introductory words or phrases.
     Example: "Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary…" (Edgar Allan Poe, *The Raven*)
   - **Non-restrictive Clauses**: Extra information (set off by commas).
     Example: "George, when a little boy, had a hatchet given him." (*The Story of Washington’s Hatchet*)
   - **Compound Sentences**: Before coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or).
     Example: "It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen." (George Orwell, *1984*)
   - **Direct Address**: To separate names or titles.
     Example: "Reader, I married him." (Charlotte Brontë, *Jane Eyre*)

6.3 Apostrophe Usage
   - Indicates possession (singular adds 's; plural ending in s adds ').
     Example: "Marley's Ghost" (Charles Dickens, *A Christmas Carol*)
   - Indicates omitted letters in contractions.
     Example: "It's a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done;" (Charles Dickens, *A Tale of Two Cities*)
   Note: Possessive pronouns (its, yours, theirs, ours, hers, his) do **not** use an apostrophe.

6.4 Capitalisation
   Capital letters mark: the first word of a sentence; proper nouns (names of people, places, institutions, days, months); the pronoun "I"; the principal words of titles; and the first word of quoted direct speech.
   Example: "Toto, I've a feeling we're not in Kansas anymore." (*The Wizard of Oz* — sentence-initial capital, proper nouns "Toto" and "Kansas", and the capital "I" in "I've")
   Primarily affects: Sentential Level

7. Common Error Patterns 

7.1 Dangling Modifiers
   A modifier that does not clearly refer to the intended word.
   Example: "Running late, the bus left without me." (incorrect)

7.2 Misplaced Modifiers
   A modifier placed too far from the word it describes.
   Example: "She served sandwiches to the children on paper plates."

7.3 Subject-Verb Agreement Errors with Collective Nouns or Indefinite Pronouns
   Example: "The team are winning." (British) vs "The team is winning." (American)

7.4 Comma Splices and Fused Sentences (Run-ons)
   The Error: Improperly joining two independent clauses. A comma splice uses only a comma, while a fused sentence uses no punctuation at all.
   Comma Splice Example: "It was raining heavily, we decided to stay inside." (incorrect)
   Fused Sentence Example: "It was raining heavily we decided to stay inside." (incorrect)
   Correction: Use a period, a semicolon, or a comma + coordinating conjunction (and, but, so).

7.5 Sentence Fragments
   The Error: Treating a phrase or a dependent clause as a complete, independent sentence. They lack either a main subject, a main verb, or a complete thought.
   Example: "Because the dog barked loudly all night." (incorrect — this is a dependent clause standing alone)
   Correction: "Because the dog barked loudly all night, we couldn't sleep."

7.6 Faulty Parallelism
   The Error: Disrupting the grammatical balance of a list, comparison, or compound structure by mixing different forms (e.g., mixing gerunds with infinitives).
   Example: "She enjoys reading, swimming, and to ride her bike." (incorrect)
   Correction: "She enjoys reading, swimming, and riding her bike." (Matches rule 5.3.1)

7.7 Ambiguous or Vague Pronoun Reference
   The Error: Using a pronoun that could refer to more than one antecedent, or using a pronoun (like it, this, or which) to refer to a vague, generalised idea rather than a specific noun.
   Ambiguous Example: "When the car hit the tree, it was badly damaged." (incorrect — was the car or the tree damaged?)
   Vague Example: "He always interrupts me, which is why I'm annoyed." (informal/vague — "which" technically has no noun antecedent; it refers to the entire previous clause)

7.8 Mixed Constructions
   The Error: Starting a sentence with one grammatical structure and shifting halfway through to an incompatible one, leaving the sentence syntactically broken.
   Example: "By studying every night is how she passed the exam." (incorrect — a prepositional phrase is awkwardly being forced to act as the subject)
   Correction: "By studying every night, she passed the exam" OR "Studying every night is how she passed the exam."

7.9 Pronoun Case Errors
   The Error: Using the wrong pronoun case (subjective vs. objective), especially in compound subjects/objects or after prepositions.
   Example: "Me and him went to the store." (incorrect) → Correction: "He and I went to the store."
   Example: "The secret is between you and I." (incorrect) → Correction: "The secret is between you and me."
   Primarily affects: Clausal & Sentential Levels

7.10 Apostrophe and Homophone Confusions
   The Error: Confusing a contraction with its possessive homophone — its/it's, your/you're, their/they're/there, whose/who's. The apostrophe form is always the contraction (it's = it is; you're = you are; who's = who is); the possessive determiners carry no apostrophe (see 6.3 note).
   Example: "The dog wagged it's tail." (incorrect) → Correction: "The dog wagged its tail."
   Test: expand the contraction — "The dog wagged it is tail" fails, so the possessive "its" is required.
   Primarily affects: Lexical Level & Orthography

7.11 Double Comparatives and Superlatives
   The Error: Combining "more/most" with an already-inflected comparative or superlative form.
   Example: "more better", "most unkindest" (non-standard — though Shakespeare uses it deliberately for emphasis: "This was the most unkindest cut of all." (*Julius Caesar*))
   Correction: "better" or "more good-natured"; one degree marker only.
   Primarily affects: Lexical & Phrasal Levels
