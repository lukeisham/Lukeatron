## 1. The Five Canons of Rhetoric

The classical five-stage workflow for transforming an initial concept into a fully realised and delivered piece of communication.

### 1.1 Inventio (Invention / Discovery)

The discovery and brainstorming phase: identifying the strongest arguments and evidence available for the subject.

**Key characteristics:** research and exploration of topics; identification of main arguments; discovery of supporting evidence; analysis of available data and sources; topic development and refinement.

#### 1.1.1 Textual Invention
- Researching multiple sources for an investigative article (source triangulation, evidence gathering)
- Gathering case law and precedents before crafting an argument (legal research, precedent analysis)
- Literature review before proposing a hypothesis (literature review, scholarly reconnaissance)
- Researching primary sources for a thesis (archival research, primary source analysis)
- Gathering evidence and witness statements (investigative discovery, testimonial collection)
- Examining documents for historical narrative (archival excavation, documentary analysis)
- Conducting interviews for biography/memoir (oral history, testimonial gathering)
- Gathering data and stakeholder input for policy (policy research, consultation process)
- Studying Scripture and commentaries for sermon (exegetical research, hermeneutical analysis)
- Stakeholder interviews and market analysis (diagnostic inquiry, competitive intelligence)
- Experimental data → patterns → inductive theory (empirical observation, pattern recognition)
- Testable predictions → theory → deductive hypothesis (hypothesis generation, falsifiable predictions)

**Worked example (textual):** A journalist investigating water quality in a regional town gathers EPA reports, interviews residents, samples data from three testing sites, and reviews comparable cases in neighbouring counties before drafting the article — this is inventio at work: the article's argument is only as strong as the source-gathering phase that precedes it.

#### 1.1.2 Visual Invention
- Mood board with reference images before production (visual research, concept development)
- Researching competitor designs and visual trends (competitive analysis, trend reconnaissance)
- Scouting locations and planning shot lists (location reconnaissance, pre-visualization)
- Artist collecting sketches and visual inspiration for a painting (ideation, source gathering)
- Gathering blueprints and spatial references for design (archival research, precedent study)

**Worked example (visual):** A filmmaker preparing a period drama collects reference images of 1920s fashion, scouts three Art Deco buildings for location shoots, and assembles a mood board of colour palettes from paintings of the era — that research phase is visual inventio, and every frame of the final film bears its imprint.

### 1.2 Dispositio (Arrangement)

The strategic arrangement of arguments or visual elements into a coherent structure to maximise impact.

**Key characteristics:** logical organisation of ideas; strategic sequencing of arguments; structural frameworks (chronological, spatial, topical); transition management; emphasis through placement.

#### 1.2.1 Textual Arrangement
- Essay: introduction, three body paragraphs, conclusion (five-paragraph structure)
- Speech: building from weakest to strongest argument (climactic order, Nestorian arrangement)
- Report: problem-solution structure (dialectical framework, IMRAD format)
- Narrative: chronological flashback technique (analepsis, non-linear sequencing)
- Analysis: compare-contrast framework (parallel structure, juxtapositional organisation)
- Legal brief: Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion (IRAC structure, forensic organisation)
- News article: most important information first, descending detail (inverted pyramid)
- Drama/play: setup, confrontation, resolution (three-act structure, Freytag's pyramid)
- Syllogistic argument: major premise, minor premise, conclusion (deductive structure)
- Sermon: textual exposition, doctrinal points, practical application (homiletic structure)

**Worked example (textual):** A lawyer's closing argument opens with the weakest piece of circumstantial evidence, moves through increasingly damning testimony, and closes with the forensic DNA match — a climactic dispositio that leaves the jury with the strongest point ringing in their ears.

#### 1.2.2 Visual Arrangement
- Poster: visual hierarchy from headline to call-to-action (hierarchical arrangement, Z-pattern layout)
- Film editing: sequential composition to build tension (escalating rhythm, shot duration decrease)
- Film editing: sequential composition to establish pacing (rhythmic editing, temporal control)
- Film editing: sequential composition to create visual continuity (eyeline match, 180-degree rule)
- Montage types: passage of time (elliptical editing), parallel action (cross-cutting), thematic juxtaposition (Eisensteinian montage), causality (Kuleshov effect)
- Transitions: match cut (metaphorical connection), jump cut (time passage), dissolve (dreamlike quality), smash cut (shock), long take (immersion), shot-reverse-shot (dialogue)
- Advanced montage: metric (mathematical cutting), rhythmic (music synchronisation), tonal (mood progression), intellectual (abstract ideas), associative (stream of consciousness)
- Website: grid systems to guide eye flow (modular grid, spatial organisation)
- Magazine: balanced text-image composition (compositional balance, asymmetrical harmony)
- Gallery curation: intentional spatial arrangement (spatial choreography, curatorial sequencing)
- Product design: ergonomic component arrangement (functional grouping, interface mapping)
- Urban planning: street grid and public space distribution (civic infrastructure, pedestrian flow)
- Book design: page layout and typography rhythm (typographic hierarchy, columnar structure)
- Wayfinding: information hierarchy and sign placement (navigational sequence, sightline optimisation)
- Retail display: merchandising zones and customer flow (store layout, focal point placement)
- Interior design: furniture arrangement and traffic patterns (spatial planning, functional zoning)
- Stage design: scenic elements and actor movement paths (blocking choreography, spatial depth)
- Photography: rule of thirds and leading lines (compositional balance, visual weight distribution)
- Data visualisation: information hierarchy and data layering (chart architecture, visual encoding)
- UI/UX design: interface elements and interaction flow (information architecture, affordance placement)
- Packaging: structural unfolding and label hierarchy (dieline planning, surface utilisation)
- Furniture: component assembly and ergonomic positioning (structural logic, proportional systems)
- Landscape design: garden paths and plant groupings (horticultural composition, viewpoint planning)
- Automotive interior: dashboard controls and ergonomic reach (cockpit layout, control clustering)

**Worked example (visual):** A movie poster places the hero's face in the upper third (dominant focal point), the title across the middle horizontal band, the antagonist's silhouette in the lower-left shadow, and the release date as the bottom-right call-to-action — every eye-movement through that Z-pattern is dispositio: the designer decided what you see first, second, and last.

### 1.3 Elocutio (Style)

The stylistic "texture" of the text or visual — precise word choice or aesthetic choices suited to the audience.

**Key characteristics:** diction and word choice; sentence variety and rhythm; tone and voice; rhetorical figures and ornaments; register and formality level. In visual rhetoric: colour palette and grading; typography and font choices; lighting style and mood; art direction and aesthetic.

#### 1.3.1 Textual Style Registers
- Academic writing: formal, precise language (scholarly register, technical diction)
- Marketing copy: vivid, emotive language (sensory vocabulary, affective diction)
- Technical documentation: clear, unambiguous terminology (plain language, denotative precision)
- Poetic writing: rich figurative language (elevated register, tropological density)
- Legal writing: exact, carefully defined diction (juridical language, lexical precision)

#### 1.3.2 Figures of Speech — Comparison
| Figure | Definition | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| 1.3.2.1 Simile | Comparison using "like" or "as" | "Her smile was as bright as the sun" |
| 1.3.2.2 Metaphor | Direct identification of unlike things | "Time is money" |
| 1.3.2.3 Analogy | Extended parallel comparison | "Just as a sword is the weapon of a warrior, a pen is the weapon of a writer" |
| 1.3.2.4 Personification | Human qualities to non-human entities | "The wind whispered through the trees" |
| 1.3.2.5 Apostrophe | Direct address to an abstract entity or absent person | "O Death, where is thy sting?" |

#### 1.3.3 Figures of Speech — Emphasis
| Figure | Definition | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| 1.3.3.1 Hyperbole | Deliberate exaggeration for effect | "I've told you a million times" |
| 1.3.3.2 Litotes | Understatement by negating the opposite | "She's not unkind" (meaning she is kind) |
| 1.3.3.3 Anaphora | Repetition of opening words or phrases | "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields..." |
| 1.3.3.4 Climax (Gradatio) | Ascending order of importance | "He came, he saw, he conquered" |
| 1.3.3.5 Antithesis | Contrasting ideas in balanced structure | "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times" |

#### 1.3.4 Figures of Speech — Wordplay
| Figure | Definition | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| 1.3.4.1 Alliteration | Repetition of initial consonant sounds | "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers" |
| 1.3.4.2 Assonance | Repetition of vowel sounds | "The rain in Spain stays mainly in the plain" |
| 1.3.4.3 Onomatopoeia | Words imitating sounds | "buzz," "hiss," "crash" |
| 1.3.4.4 Pun / Paronomasia | Wordplay exploiting multiple meanings | "I used to be a banker, but I lost interest" |
| 1.3.4.5 Chiasmus | Inverted parallel structure | "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country" |

#### 1.3.5 Figures of Speech — Substitution
| Figure | Definition | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| 1.3.5.1 Metonymy | Substituting an associated term | "The White House announced..." (building for the administration) |
| 1.3.5.2 Synecdoche | Part representing whole, or vice versa | "All hands on deck" (hands for sailors) |
| 1.3.5.3 Euphemism | Mild expression replacing a harsh one | "passed away" for "died" |
| 1.3.5.4 Irony | Meaning opposite to the literal statement | "What a beautiful day" (said during a thunderstorm) |
| 1.3.5.5 Oxymoron | Contradictory terms combined | "deafening silence," "bittersweet" |

#### 1.3.6 Visual Style — Genre Conventions
- Film Noir: high-contrast black and white (chiaroscuro lighting, monochromatic palette)
- Western: wide-angle desert vistas and golden hour (frontier iconography, expansive framing)
- Science Fiction: cool palette (technological aesthetic, speculative mise-en-scène)
- Horror: low-key lighting and desaturated colours (atmospheric tension, shadow manipulation)
- Comedy: bright high-key lighting and saturated colours (optimistic palette, visual clarity)
- Drama: naturalistic lighting and muted grading (realist aesthetic, emotional authenticity)
- Action: dynamic camera movement (kinetic cinematography, high-contrast composition)
- Thriller: claustrophobic framing (psychological disorientation, paranoid aesthetics)
- Romance: soft focus and warm colour temperature (diffused lighting, romantic filter)
- Musical: choreographed camera (spectacle aesthetic, Technicolor palette)
- Documentary: handheld camera and natural lighting (vérité style, observational framing)
- Animation: stylised colour (non-photorealistic rendering, graphic design)
- Fantasy: rich production design (mythopoeic imagery, otherworldly palette)
- Crime: gritty urban palette and amber street lighting (neo-noir aesthetic, urban decay)
- War: desaturated combat footage (documentary aesthetic, visceral framing)
- Historical Epic: grand scope and period-accurate colour (sweeping vistas, costume pageantry)
- Superhero: CGI-enhanced action and primary colours (comic-book aesthetic, dynamic composition)
- Psychological Thriller: subjective camera (unreliable perspective, mental distortion)
- Mockumentary: low-budget aesthetic (faux-documentary style, deadpan presentation)
- Arthouse/Experimental: unconventional framing (non-narrative aesthetics, auteur vision)

#### 1.3.7 Visual Style — Auteur Signatures
- Wes Anderson: symmetrical framing, pastel colour grading
- Brand identity: colour palette and typeface system (visual language system, brand semiotics)
- Instagram filter: consistent aesthetic across images (chromatic unity, tonal treatment)
- Minimalist design: clean lines and white space (reductive aesthetic, negative space composition)

**Worked example (textual):** Compare "The meeting ended" (plain) with "The deliberations drew to a close" (formal register, elevated diction). Both convey the same event, but elocutio — the choice of words and register — positions the speaker differently in relation to the audience.

**Worked example (visual):** The same scene shot in two different elocutio registers: a breakup conversation lit with warm golden-hour light and soft focus (romantic register — suggests nostalgia, tenderness) vs. the same dialogue shot in cold blue light with harsh shadows and handheld camera (thriller register — suggests alienation, threat). The elocutio IS the meaning.

### 1.4 Memoria (Memory)

The use of mnemonic systems and cognitive techniques to internalise content for fluid, natural delivery. In textual rhetoric: repeated motifs, catchphrases, mnemonic devices. In visual rhetoric: recurring visual motifs, brand consistency, iconic callbacks.

**Key characteristics:** memory aids and techniques; internalisation of structure; recall mechanisms; cognitive frameworks; thematic consistency.

#### 1.4.1 Textual Memory Techniques
- Speaker: method of loci to remember speech points (memory palace, spatial mnemonics)
- Novel: repeated motifs creating thematic coherence (thematic recurrence, symbolic leitmotif)
- Educational content: embedded mnemonic acronyms (mnemonic devices, memory aids)
- Film series: recurring verbal catchphrases (verbal signature, catchphrase motif)
- Musical composition: leitmotifs for character themes (musical memoria, thematic recall)

#### 1.4.2 Visual Memory Techniques
- Film series: consistent colour motifs (e.g., blue in The Matrix) (chromatic leitmotif, visual callback)
- Corporate branding: logo and colour consistency (brand consistency, identity persistence)
- Director: signature visual style across multiple films (auteur continuity, stylistic fingerprint)
- Graphic novel: recurring visual symbols (symbolic recurrence, iconographic motif)
- Theme park: consistent iconography for wayfinding (semiotic system, navigational consistency)

**Worked example (textual):** A novelist plants the image of a locked garden gate in chapter one, returns to it in chapter seven when the protagonist recalls a childhood memory, and opens it in the final chapter as the character faces the truth. The repetition isn't accident — it's memoria: the gate becomes a thematic anchor the reader carries through the whole book.

**Worked example (visual):** In *The Matrix*, the green tint of scenes inside the simulation and the blue tint of the real world are never explained in dialogue — they're visual memoria. By the third act, the audience registers location instinctively from the colour grade alone.

### 1.5 Pronuntiatio (Delivery / Performance)

The final execution of the message — physical appearance, vocal modulation, gesture, or the technical format and platform of delivery.

**Key characteristics:** genre conventions; medium-specific formatting; presentation style; performance elements; publication/delivery method. In visual rhetoric: technical quality and resolution; exhibition format and platform; audio-visual synchronisation; distribution method.

#### 1.5.1 Textual Delivery Modes
- TED talk: confident body language and vocal variation (kinetic rhetoric, prosodic modulation)
- Poem: performed with attention to rhythm and strategic pauses (caesura, rhythmic delivery)
- Business presentation: slides and clear speaking (multimodal delivery, corporate register)
- Courtroom argument: delivered with authoritative tone (forensic delivery, authoritative register)
- Sermon: passionate vocal dynamics (homiletic delivery, emotional inflection)

#### 1.5.2 Visual Delivery Modes
- Film in IMAX format: maximum visual impact (large-format exhibition, immersive presentation)
- Digital advertisement: optimised for mobile viewing (platform optimisation, responsive design)
- Gallery exhibition: careful lighting and placement (installation design, spatial presentation)
- 4K video broadcast: high-definition television (technical specification, resolution quality)
- Premium paper stock: tactile quality (material selection, haptic consideration)

**Worked example (textual):** The same sermon manuscript delivered two ways: read monotone from a lectern vs. delivered from memory with pauses, eye contact, and vocal variation. The words are identical; the pronuntiatio determines whether the congregation hears a lecture or a proclamation.

**Worked example (visual):** A photograph viewed on a phone screen vs. the same image printed at gallery scale with professional lighting — pronuntiatio transforms the viewing experience without altering a single pixel of the image.

## 2. The Three Rhetorical Appeals (Proofs)

The primary modes of persuasion: establishing authority (ethos), evoking emotion (pathos), and demonstrating reason (logos).

### 2.1 Ethos (Credibility / Character)

Establishing credibility and trust by demonstrating the speaker's reliable character, expertise, and moral standing.

**Key characteristics:** demonstrating expertise and knowledge; citing authoritative sources; showing good moral character; building trust with the audience; presenting credentials or experience.

#### 2.1.1 Textual Ethos
- Doctor citing medical research and professional experience
- Witness testimony demonstrating personal reliability
- Author establishing credentials in a preface or biography
- Expert opinion backed by years of study
- Professional certification displayed to establish authority

#### 2.1.2 Visual Ethos
- Corporate video using high-end production and professional talent
- Doctor wearing a white coat and stethoscope in medical advertising
- News graphics using official logos and professional design
- Award badges and certifications displayed on product packaging
- Expert testimonial with credentials shown on screen

**Worked example:** A financial advisor's newsletter opens with "CFA charterholder, 15 years managing institutional portfolios" before making any recommendation. The credentials don't prove the advice is correct — they establish ethos: "this person has earned the right to be heard on this topic."

---

### 2.2 Pathos (Emotion)

Generating engagement by appealing to the audience's emotions, creating a visceral or empathetic connection.

**Key characteristics:** evoking emotional responses; creating empathy and identification; using vivid, sensory language; telling stories or anecdotes; appealing to values and beliefs.

#### 2.2.1 Textual Pathos
- Charity advertisements showing suffering to motivate donations
- Memorial speech evoking grief and remembrance
- Political rhetoric appealing to hope, fear, or anger
- Heartwarming personal story creating empathy
- Tragic narrative evoking sorrow and compassion

#### 2.2.2 Visual Pathos
- Charity campaign showing images of suffering to evoke compassion
- Sunset imagery in romantic films creating warmth and nostalgia
- Dark, desaturated colours in horror films evoking fear
- Close-up of a crying child in a humanitarian advertisement
- Victory celebration photo capturing joy and triumph

**Worked example:** A fundraising email doesn't open with statistics about hunger — it opens with "Mariam is seven. Last night she went to bed hungry for the 412th consecutive day." The statistic might come later; pathos gets the reader to care enough to keep reading.

---

### 2.3 Logos (Logic / Reason)

Building credibility through logical reasoning, presentation of data, and structured evidence to support a rational conclusion.

**Key characteristics:** logical argumentation; evidence and data; clear reasoning chains; factual accuracy; cause-and-effect relationships.

#### 2.3.1 Textual Logos
- Scientific paper presenting data analysis and conclusions
- Legal brief using precedents and logical reasoning
- Financial report showing statistical trends and projections
- Mathematical proof demonstrating a theorem step by step
- Empirical study presenting evidence-based findings

#### 2.3.2 Visual Logos
- Infographic showing statistical trends with clear graphs
- Tutorial video demonstrating a step-by-step process
- Before-and-after images demonstrating product effectiveness
- Data visualisation mapping complex information clearly
- Flowchart illustrating a logical decision-making process

**Worked example:** An op-ed arguing for a policy change doesn't rely on anecdotes — it cites three peer-reviewed studies, a cost-benefit table, and a counterargument that it addresses and refutes. That's logos: the audience is persuaded because the reasoning holds up to scrutiny, not because they like the author or feel moved.

## 3. Rhetorical Tropes

Non-literal ways of shifting meaning to engage the audience's imagination. Organised by the type of semantic operation performed.

### 3.1 Resemblance (Metaphor / Simile)

Figures that create meaning by comparing two distinct things based on shared traits.

**Key characteristics:** comparison of unlike things; "like" or "as" (simile) vs. direct equation (metaphor); creates new understanding through analogy; transfers qualities from one domain to another.

#### 3.1.1 Textual Resemblance
- "Time is money" (metaphor)
- "Her smile was as bright as the sun" (simile)
- "The world is a stage" (metaphor)
- "He has a heart of stone" (metaphor)
- "Drowning in work" (metaphor)

#### 3.1.2 Visual Resemblance
- Salvador Dalí's melting clocks (time as fluid/unstable)
- Person's head replaced with a lightbulb (representing ideas)
- Hands forming a dove shape (peace through unity)
- Tree with roots becoming a brain (nature of thought)
- Hourglass with sand forming a cityscape (passage of civilisation)

**Worked example:** "The CEO stalked the boardroom like a lion surveying its territory." The comparison transfers predator-prey dynamics into corporate space — the metaphor does argumentative work that a literal description ("The CEO walked confidently") cannot.

---

### 3.2 Association (Metonymy)

Figures that substitute a word or image for something closely related to it.

**Key characteristics:** substitution based on close relationship; part-whole; cause-effect; container-content.

#### 3.2.1 Textual Association
- "The White House announced..." (building for presidential administration)
- "The pen is mightier than the sword" (tools for writing and warfare)
- "Silicon Valley leads innovation" (place for the tech industry)
- "The crown ordered the execution" (crown for the monarch)
- "Wall Street reacted negatively" (street for the financial sector)

#### 3.2.2 Visual Association
- Gavel representing justice/law
- Crown representing monarchy/royalty
- Eiffel Tower representing Paris/France
- Stethoscope representing the medical profession
- Briefcase representing the business/corporate world

**Worked example:** A news headline reads "Downing Street rejected the proposal." The street didn't literally reject anything — metonymy substitutes the location for the prime minister's office, compressing a complex institutional action into two words the reader instantly understands.

---

### 3.3 Naming (Synecdoche)

Figures where a part represents the whole, or the whole represents a part.

**Key characteristics:** part for whole or whole for part; specific for general; material for object; creates focus or emphasis.

#### 3.3.1 Textual Naming
- "All hands on deck" (hands for sailors)
- "Nice wheels!" (wheels for car)
- "The law arrived" (law for police)
- "They hired some new brains" (brains for intelligent people)
- "He's just a hired gun" (gun for mercenary/specialist)

#### 3.3.2 Visual Naming
- Showing only feet walking to represent a journey
- Close-up of hands shaking to represent agreement
- Just the wheels of a car to represent transportation
- Single raised fist representing a protest movement
- Crown of head showing grey hair to represent aging

**Worked example:** A filmmaker shows only a hand turning a door handle, then cut to the character inside the room. The audience never sees the full body entering — synecdoche uses the part (the hand on the handle) to stand for the whole action, trusting the viewer to complete the picture.

---

### 3.4 Invention (Neologism / Creative Language)

The creative stretching of language or visual grammar — coining new words, repurposing parts of speech, or breaking visual conventions.

#### 3.4.1 Textual Invention
- "Let's Google that" (noun → verb)
- "I need to unfriend them" (creative combination)
- "Adulting is hard" (noun → gerund)
- "Stop ghosting me" (noun → verb)
- "Brexit" (portmanteau: Britain + exit)

#### 3.4.2 Visual Invention
- Glitch art using digital artefacts creatively
- Abstract expressionism breaking traditional representation
- M.C. Escher's impossible architecture
- Surreal photo manipulation merging impossible elements
- Non-naturalistic colour grading creating an alien atmosphere

**Worked example (textual):** "He Venmoed me the money." The brand name becomes a verb — a neologism that didn't exist twenty years ago but is now instantly understood. Invention keeps language alive by stretching its categories.

---

### 3.5 Extended Meaning (Allegory / Irony / Sustained Trope)

Figures where the non-literal meaning is sustained across an entire work or passage, not confined to a single phrase or image.

#### 3.5.1 Textual Extended Meaning
- George Orwell's *Animal Farm* (political allegory)
- Saying "What lovely weather" during a storm (irony)
- Biblical parables (allegorical teaching)
- Swift's "A Modest Proposal" (sustained satirical irony)
- Plato's Allegory of the Cave (philosophical allegory)

#### 3.5.2 Visual Extended Meaning
- Hieronymus Bosch paintings with sustained moral allegories
- Political cartoon with layered symbolic narrative
- Music video with sustained metaphorical storyline
- Banksy street art with hidden social commentary
- Advertising campaign with recurring symbolic motif

**Worked example:** Swift's "A Modest Proposal" doesn't contain a single ironic line — the entire essay is an extended irony, proposing cannibalism as a rational economic solution to poverty. The meaning lives not in any one sentence but in the sustained gap between what is said and what is meant, which the reader must bridge.

---

### 3.6 Ornamental Epithet

A descriptive label or stylistic signature that becomes attached to a person, character, or creator.

#### 3.6.1 Textual Epithets
- "Swift-footed Achilles" (Homeric epithet)
- "The Great Emancipator" for Abraham Lincoln (historical epithet)
- "The rosy-fingered dawn" (Homeric epithet)
- "The Iron Lady" for Margaret Thatcher (political epithet)
- "The King of Pop" for Michael Jackson (cultural epithet)

#### 3.6.2 Visual Epithets (Auteur Signatures)
- Wes Anderson's symmetrical framing and pastel palettes
- Michael Bay's "Bayhem" explosion aesthetics
- Banksy's stencil-art signature style
- Hitchcock's suspenseful low-angle shots
- Tarantino's trunk-shot perspective

**Worked example:** "Swift-footed Achilles" appears dozens of times in the *Iliad* — it's never a fresh description, always a formula. The epithet functions as memoria (the audience recognises the character instantly) and as elocutio (the dactylic rhythm fits the hexameter line). What looks like repetition is actually precision engineering.

## 4. Rhetorical Fallacies

Systematic errors in reasoning, organised by the type of logical failure. Each fallacy is presented with its definition, an example of its misuse, and — where applicable — a legitimate use of the same pattern (since many fallacy forms also underlie valid reasoning when properly constrained).

### 4.1 Fallacies of Relevance

Arguments where the premises are logically irrelevant to the conclusion — the reasoning fails because it addresses the wrong thing.

| ID | Fallacy | Definition | Misuse Example | Legitimate Use |
|----|---------|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| 4.1.1 | Ad Hominem | Attacking the person making the argument rather than the argument itself | "You can't trust his climate data — he drives an SUV" | Vetting source credibility when character is directly relevant (e.g., perjury conviction for a witness) |
| 4.1.2 | Tu Quoque | Dismissing an argument because the arguer is hypocritical | "You tell me to stop smoking, but you smoke yourself" | Identifying lack of shared values as grounds for not taking advice |
| 4.1.3 | Genetic Fallacy | Judging a claim solely by its origin rather than its merit | "That idea came from a tabloid, so it's false" | Understanding the historical context of a claim to evaluate its formation |
| 4.1.4 | Appeal to Authority | Citing an authority figure outside their field of expertise | "This celebrity says the vaccine is dangerous" | Relying on vetted expertise within the authority's domain (e.g., a cardiologist on heart disease) |
| 4.1.5 | Appeal to Emotion | Manipulating feelings to bypass rational evaluation | "If you don't support this policy, you don't care about children" | Building empathy as motivation for justice (emotion paired with reason) |
| 4.1.6 | Appeal to Pity | Relying solely on sympathy instead of evidence | "I should pass this course because my grandmother is ill" | Humanising a situation for compassionate policy-making alongside evidence |
| 4.1.7 | Appeal to Force | Using threats to coerce agreement | "Agree with me or I'll make your life difficult" | Enforcing safety boundaries (e.g., "trespassers will be prosecuted") |
| 4.1.8 | Irrelevant Conclusion (Ignoratio Elenchi) | An argument that proves something other than the claim at issue | "This policy is bad because the opposition proposed it" (doesn't engage the policy itself) | Reframing a discussion toward a more productive topic |
| 4.1.9 | Straw Man | Misrepresenting an opponent's argument to make it easier to attack | "You want to reduce military spending? So you want to leave the country defenceless?" | Simplifying a complex argument for educational purposes (with acknowledgment) |
| 4.1.10 | Red Herring | Introducing an irrelevant topic to distract from the real issue | "Yes, the budget has a deficit, but look at this scandal involving the treasurer's personal life" | Pivoting away from an unproductive tangent to return to the core issue |
| 4.1.11 | Poisoning the Well | Discrediting a source before they've spoken | "Before my opponent speaks, you should know he was fired from his last job" | Warning an audience about a known bad-faith actor with evidence |
| 4.1.12 | Guilt by Association | Rejecting a claim because it's associated with a disliked person or group | "That scientific finding was cited by a controversial group, so the finding must be wrong" | Evaluating company kept for safety or reputation when the association is the point at issue |

### 4.2 Fallacies of Ambiguity

Arguments that rely on ambiguous language — a word, phrase, or grammatical structure shifts meaning mid-argument.

| ID | Fallacy | Definition | Misuse Example | Legitimate Use |
|----|---------|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| 4.2.1 | Equivocation | Using a word with two different meanings in the same argument as if it had one | "Only man is rational. No woman is a man. Therefore no woman is rational." (shifts "man" = "humanity" → "man" = "male") | Creative wordplay or punning for literary effect |
| 4.2.2 | Amphiboly | Ambiguous grammatical structure that supports multiple interpretations | "I saw the man with the telescope" (did I use a telescope, or did the man have one?) | Broadly worded laws allowing judicial discretion |
| 4.2.3 | Accent | Shifting meaning by emphasising different words | "I never said she stole the money" (stress different words → seven different meanings) | Highlighting nuance to clarify which claim is being asserted or denied |
| 4.2.4 | Composition | Assuming what is true of the parts is true of the whole | "Each player on this team is a star, so this must be a great team" (ignores chemistry and fit) | Building a strong system from individually excellent parts where the system is genuinely additive |
| 4.2.5 | Division | Assuming what is true of the whole is true of each part | "This company is profitable, so every department must be profitable" | Applying organisational standards consistently across divisions |
| 4.2.6 | Etymological Fallacy | Claiming a word's original or oldest meaning is its "true" meaning | "Decimate literally means to kill one in ten, so you can't use it to mean 'devastate'" | Using linguistic history to enrich understanding (descriptive, not prescriptive) |
| 4.2.7 | Quoting out of Context | Removing a statement from its surrounding context to change its meaning | Movie review: "The worst film...if you're looking for a masterpiece. For a fun night out, it delivers." → quoted as: "The worst film" | Using representative soundbites to convey core themes (with transparent sourcing) |

### 4.3 Fallacies of Presumption

Arguments whose premises assume the very point at issue, or make an unwarranted leap that the evidence doesn't support.

| ID | Fallacy | Definition | Misuse Example | Legitimate Use |
|----|---------|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| 4.3.1 | Begging the Question (Petitio Principii) | The conclusion is restated in the premises | "This law is constitutional because it doesn't violate the constitution" | Establishing fundamental axioms in a formal system where they are explicitly declared |
| 4.3.2 | Complex Question | A question that embeds an unproven assumption | "Have you stopped cheating on your taxes?" (assumes past cheating) | Directing a conversation toward a core issue when the framing premise is acknowledged |
| 4.3.3 | False Dilemma | Presenting only two options when more exist | "You're either with us or against us" | Forcing a decision when time is genuinely constrained and the binary is real |
| 4.3.4 | Special Pleading | Making an exception to a general rule without justification | "The law applies to everyone, but my client's situation is different" (without stating why) | Recognising genuinely unique circumstances that warrant a departure |
| 4.3.5 | No True Scotsman | Redefining a category to exclude counterexamples ad hoc | "No Scotsman puts sugar on his porridge." "But Angus does." "Well, no TRUE Scotsman does." | Defining ideal standards for aspirational purposes (explicitly marked as ideals) |
| 4.3.6 | Moving the Goalposts | Demanding new or different evidence after the original standard has been met | "You proved the policy saves money." "But does it save enough money?" → "But does it also improve morale?" | Increasing standards appropriately as a field matures and baseline competence rises |
| 4.3.7 | Burden of Proof | Claiming a proposition is true because it hasn't been disproven | "You can't prove aliens don't exist, so they must exist" | Encouraging open-mindedness about unfalsifiable possibilities (without asserting truth) |
| 4.3.8 | Loaded Question / Language | Phrasing that forces a particular answer or embeds a value judgement | "How can you support this destructive policy?" (assumes the policy is destructive) | Taking a clear stance on a contested issue while inviting response |
| 4.3.9 | False Cause (Non Causa Pro Causa) | Assuming causation without evidence | "I wore my lucky socks and we won — the socks caused the win" | Identifying potential correlations as candidates for further testing |
| 4.3.10 | Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc | "After this, therefore because of this" | "The rooster crows before sunrise, so the rooster causes the sun to rise" | Noticing temporal sequences as heuristics for investigation |
| 4.3.11 | Cum Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc | Correlation presented as causation | "Ice cream sales and drowning rates rise together — ice cream causes drowning" (both rise in summer) | Using observed trends as tentative leads for controlled study |
| 4.3.12 | Slippery Slope | Asserting that one step will inevitably lead to a chain of increasingly dire consequences without evidence | "If we allow this minor regulation, next they'll control every aspect of our lives" | Applying a precautionary principle where there is genuine evidence of cascading effects |
| 4.3.13 | Circular Reasoning | X is true because of Y; Y is true because of X | "The Bible is true because it says it's the word of God; it's the word of God because the Bible says so" | Self-reinforcing logical systems where axioms are acknowledged and the system is evaluated for internal consistency |

### 4.4 Statistical and Generalisation Fallacies

Errors in drawing conclusions from data — samples too small, trends misread, probabilities misunderstood.

| ID | Fallacy | Definition | Misuse Example | Legitimate Use |
|----|---------|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| 4.4.1 | Hasty Generalisation | Drawing a conclusion from a sample too small to support it | "I met two rude Parisians, so all Parisians are rude" | Rapid heuristics for low-stakes decisions where speed matters more than accuracy |
| 4.4.2 | Sweeping Generalisation | Applying a general rule to an exceptional case without adjustment | "Cutting people with knives is assault — therefore surgeons are criminals" | Consistency for fairness in routine cases where exceptions are genuinely rare |
| 4.4.3 | Anecdotal Evidence | Using a personal story to dismiss systematic data | "My grandfather smoked a pack a day and lived to 95, so smoking can't be that dangerous" | Humanising data points to make statistical patterns emotionally accessible |
| 4.4.4 | Texas Sharpshooter | Cherry-picking data clusters that fit a conclusion, ignoring the rest | Drawing a bullseye around bullet holes after shooting, then claiming accuracy | Finding patterns as a hypothesis-generation exercise (before testing) |
| 4.4.5 | Gambler's Fallacy | Believing past independent events affect future probability | "I've flipped heads five times — tails must be due" (coin has no memory) | Recognising when a system is genuinely due for a reset (only where independence does NOT hold) |
| 4.4.6 | Survivorship Bias | Focusing only on those who survived a selection process, ignoring those who didn't | "All successful entrepreneurs dropped out of college, so dropping out leads to success" (ignoring the far larger number who dropped out and failed) | Studying success stories to identify shared traits, while acknowledging the selection bias |
| 4.4.7 | Appeal to Popularity (Ad Populum) | Claiming something is true because many people believe it | "Millions of people believe in astrology, so there must be something to it" | Social proof as a trust heuristic for community or consumer decisions |
| 4.4.8 | Appeal to Tradition (Ad Antiquitatem) | Claiming something is right because it's always been done that way | "We've always held the meeting on Tuesday, so we shouldn't change it" | Preserving cultural wisdom that encodes hard-won practical knowledge |
| 4.4.9 | Appeal to Nature | Claiming something is good or right because it's "natural" | "This herbal remedy is natural, so it must be safe and effective" (natural ≠ safe: hemlock is natural) | Preferring sustainable, bio-aligned solutions where nature genuinely offers a superior model |
| 4.4.10 | Middle Ground | Assuming the truth lies between two extreme positions | "You say 2+2=4, she says 2+2=6 — the truth must be 5" | Negotiating compromise in genuinely subjective or value-laden disputes |

### 4.5 Formal Fallacies (Deductive)

Errors in the logical structure of a deductive argument — the form itself is invalid, regardless of content.

| ID | Fallacy | Form | Definition | Valid Counterpart |
|----|---------|------|-----------|-------------------|
| 4.5.1 | Affirming the Consequent | If P then Q; Q; therefore P | Reversing a conditional: the truth of the consequence doesn't prove the truth of the antecedent | Modus Ponens: If P then Q; P; therefore Q |
| 4.5.2 | Denying the Antecedent | If P then Q; Not P; therefore Not Q | Negating the condition doesn't negate the result — Q could have other causes | Modus Tollens: If P then Q; Not Q; therefore Not P |
| 4.5.3 | Affirming a Disjunct | P or Q; P; therefore Not Q | Assumes "or" is exclusive (XOR) when it may be inclusive | Valid only when "or" is established as exclusive |
| 4.5.4 | Denying a Conjunct | Not both P and Q; Not P; therefore Q | From "not both," the absence of one doesn't guarantee the presence of the other — both could be absent | Valid only when at least one is independently established |
| 4.5.5 | Fallacy Fallacy | Argued poorly; therefore the conclusion is false | Rejecting a conclusion because the argument for it contains a fallacy — the conclusion might still be true | Insisting on rigorous argumentation: "this argument fails; we need a better one" |
| 4.5.6 | Personal Incredulity | I don't understand X; therefore X is false | Confusing personal incomprehension with objective impossibility | Identifying that an explanation is genuinely lacking — a legitimate trigger for further inquiry |
| 4.5.7 | Sunk Cost Fallacy | Continuing an investment because resources have already been spent | "We've spent $10 million on this project — we can't stop now" (the money is gone regardless) | Showing perseverance where the past investment genuinely increases the likelihood of future success |
| 4.5.8 | Existential Fallacy | Assuming a universal statement implies the existence of members | "All unicorns have horns" — true in logic, but doesn't prove unicorns exist | Exploring "what if" theoretical spaces without asserting real-world existence |

**Worked example (Formal):** "If it rains, the ground will be wet. The ground is wet. Therefore it rained." This is affirming the consequent (4.5.1). The ground could be wet because a sprinkler ran. The form is invalid even though the conclusion might happen to be true.

**Worked example (Informal):** "You say we should cut the defence budget, but if we do that, our enemies will see weakness, which will lead to an attack, which will lead to war, and before you know it we've lost our sovereignty." This is a slippery slope (4.3.12) — each link in the chain asserts inevitability without evidence. The response should be: "Show me the mechanism by which each step follows from the last."
