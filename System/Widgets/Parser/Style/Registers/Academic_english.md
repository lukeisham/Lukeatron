# Style — Academic English

Extracted from [Style_content.md](../Style_content.md) §6 — an independent register
sweep. Selected and run on its own, never combined with the Clarity/Brevity/
Coherence/Voice/Diction genre sweeps or their antitheses (Obscurity/Verbosity/
Incoherence/Affectation/Ornament) — it has no opposite toggle; it always checks
for this one register.

Describing results requires strict factual objectivity; discussing interpretations
relies on calibrated certainty to align claims with evidence. Editorial synthesis
of standard academic-writing convention — no direct quotation, no single named
source. §6.1–6.6 govern describing results (data reporting); §6.7–6.10 govern
discussing interpretations (analysis and inference).

### 6.1 Tense precision

Past tense for methodology and completed findings; present tense reserved for
established facts or for discussing figures and tables.

Example: "We measured cortisol levels at three time points." / "Figure 2 illustrates the dose-response curve."

### 6.2 Quantification over qualification

Subjective qualifiers ("large increase," "often," "significant" without
statistical backing) are prohibited. Central tendencies (mean, median) are
paired with a measure of dispersion (standard deviation, interquartile range,
or confidence interval).

Example: "The mean score increased by 4.2 points (SD = 1.1)" rather than "scores increased significantly."

### 6.3 Non-evaluative language

Value-laden adjectives (dramatic, surprising, disappointing) are eliminated.
Findings are stated neutrally.

Example: "The parameter decreased by 14%" rather than "The parameter saw a concerning drop."

### 6.4 Precise relational verbs

Mathematically descriptive verbs (plateaued, diverged, fluctuated, monotonically
increased) replace generic motion verbs (went up, shifted, changed).

Example: "Response rates plateaued after week six" rather than "Response rates changed after week six."

### 6.5 Dual absolute and relative framing

Percentages are paired with absolute values to prevent misleading
contextualisation.

Example: "Increased by 50% (from 2 to 3 participants)" rather than "Increased by 50%."

### 6.6 Agentless, data-centric framing

Results reporting keeps the analytical focus on the data, not the researchers.

Example: "An inverse relationship was observed between X and Y" rather than "We found an inverse relationship between X and Y."

### 6.7 Epistemic hedging

Modal verbs (may, might, could) and tentative verbs (suggests, indicates,
appears to align with) express degrees of certainty without claiming absolute
proof.

Example: "These results suggest that X may moderate Y."

### 6.8 Causal versus associative distinction

Causal verbs (causes, drives, induces) are reserved for controlled experimental
or causal-inference designs. Associative framing (is correlated with, co-varies
with, is associated with) is used for observational or cross-sectional data.

Example: "X is associated with Y" (observational) versus "X induced a reduction in Y" (controlled experiment).

### 6.9 Boundary and scope framing

Concessive clauses explicitly mark the limits of an interpretation.

Example: "While these results demonstrate X in model A, direct application to system B requires further validation."

### 6.10 Attribution and epistemic distance

New findings are framed against the existing literature, marking clearly where
established fact ends and new hypothesis begins.

Example: "In contrast to the linear framework established by Lee et al., these observations point toward a threshold effect."
