# Systematic Theology — Content Source

This file serves as the doctrinal knowledge base for the Systematic Theology parser aide. It supplies the aide with structured taxonomies of systematic theology loci, the Reformed *ordo salutis*, the doctrines of grace (TULIP), and a theme-frequency scoring method for evaluating the doctrinal weight of a text.

---

## 1. PART A — Systematic Theology Topic Map

The classic Reformed organization of systematic theology proceeds from first principles (revelation and method) through the full sweep of God's work: God in Himself, God's decree, creation, fall, redemption, the church, and the consummation. The following eight major loci are standard across the Reformed tradition.

### 1.1 Prolegomena (First Principles)

The introductory locus that establishes the nature, sources, and method of theology.

**1.1.1 The Nature of Theology**
- What is theology, and how does it relate to faith, piety, and doxology?
- The distinction between *theologia archetypa* (God's self-knowledge) and *theologia ectypa* (creaturely knowledge of God).
- Whether theology is a theoretical science, a practical wisdom, or both (*sapientia*).

**1.1.2 Revelation**
- *General revelation*: God's self-disclosure in creation, providence, and conscience. Key passages: Psalm 19:1–4; Romans 1:19–20.
- *Special revelation*: God's self-disclosure in redemptive history, culminating in Christ and inscribed in Scripture.
- The relationship between general and special revelation: nature as a true but insufficient witness; Scripture as the necessary and normative witness.

**1.1.3 Scripture**
- *Inspiration*: Verbal, plenary inspiration — the Spirit so superintended the human authors that their words are truly God's words. 2 Timothy 3:16–17; 2 Peter 1:20–21.
- *Authority*: Scripture as the supreme norm (*norma normans non normata*) for faith and life.
- *Necessity*: Scripture is necessary for saving knowledge of God; general revelation is not salvific.
- *Clarity (Perspicuity)*: The central message of salvation is plain in Scripture; difficult passages do not obscure the way of salvation.
- *Sufficiency*: Scripture contains all that is necessary for faith and godliness; it does not require supplementation by tradition or new revelation.
- *Canon*: The recognition of the 66 books as the rule of faith. Questions of canonicity vs. authority (does the church authorise the canon, or does the canon authorise the church?).

**1.1.4 Theological Method**
- The relationship of reason to revelation: *fides quaerens intellectum* (faith seeking understanding). Anselm's dictum, affirmed across the Reformed tradition.
- The use of philosophy, creeds, and confessions as subordinate standards.
- The hermeneutical spiral: text → interpretation → theological synthesis → re-engagement with text.
- The *analogy of faith* (*analogia fidei*): Scripture interprets Scripture; clearer passages illuminate obscure ones.

### 1.2 Theology Proper (The Doctrine of God)

The doctrine of God as He is in Himself and in His works. Bavinck devotes the whole second volume of his *Reformed Dogmatics* to this locus.

**1.2.1 The Knowability and Incomprehensibility of God**
- God is knowable truly but never exhaustively (the Creator–creature distinction).
- The *via triplex*: the way of affirmation, negation, and eminence in speaking of God.
- Accommodation: God condescends to reveal Himself in creaturely forms and language. Calvin's *Institutes* I.13.1 is the classical statement.

**1.2.2 The Names of God**
- OT names: *YHWH* (Exodus 3:14: "I AM WHO I AM"), *Elohim*, *Adonai*, *El Shaddai*.
- NT names: *Theos*, *Kyrios*, *Pater* (Father), particularly as Jesus taught His disciples to address God.

**1.2.3 The Attributes of God**
- *Incommunicable attributes* (those God does not share with creatures):
  - Aseity (self-existence): God's being is from Himself. Exodus 3:14.
  - Immutability (unchangeableness): Malachi 3:6; James 1:17.
  - Infinity and Eternity: Psalm 90:2; 1 Timothy 1:17.
  - Simplicity: God is not composed of parts; His attributes are identical with His essence.
  - Omnipresence: Psalm 139:7–10.
  - Omniscience: Psalm 139:1–4.
  - Omnipotence: Genesis 17:1; Jeremiah 32:17; Matthew 19:26.
- *Communicable attributes* (those God shares analogically with creatures):
  - Holiness: Isaiah 6:3; Revelation 4:8.
  - Righteousness and Justice: Deuteronomy 32:4; Romans 3:25–26.
  - Goodness, Love, Grace, Mercy: Exodus 34:6–7; 1 John 4:8.
  - Truth and Faithfulness: Numbers 23:19; Titus 1:2.
  - Wrath: Romans 1:18; John 3:36.

**1.2.4 The Trinity**
- The biblical basis: there is one God (Deuteronomy 6:4; 1 Corinthians 8:4–6), yet the Father is God (1 Peter 1:2), the Son is God (John 1:1; John 20:28; Colossians 2:9), and the Spirit is God (Acts 5:3–4; 1 Corinthians 3:16).
- The three persons are distinct: Matthew 28:19 (singular "name," three persons); 2 Corinthians 13:14 (trinitarian benediction); John 14:16–17 (the Son sends the Spirit from the Father).
- *Ontological Trinity*: the eternal relations of origin — the Father begets, the Son is begotten, the Spirit proceeds.
- *Economic Trinity*: the distinct roles of the persons in creation and redemption.
- Historical development: Nicea (325), Constantinople (381), the Athanasian Creed. Key terms: *homoousios*, *hypostasis*, *perichoresis*.

**1.2.5 The Decrees of God**
- The eternal plan by which God ordains whatsoever comes to pass. Ephesians 1:11.
- Relationship to creation and providence: decree is the eternal act; creation and providence are its temporal execution.
- The distinction between God's *decretive will* (what He determines) and *preceptive will* (what He commands creatures to do).
- The problem of evil and the distinction between primary and secondary causation.

**1.2.6 Creation**
- Creation *ex nihilo* (out of nothing): Genesis 1:1; Hebrews 11:3.
- The goodness of creation: Genesis 1:31.
- The six days: interpretative questions (calendar-day view, day-age view, analogical-day view, framework view, etc.).
- Angels: created spiritual beings, elect and fallen. Limited scriptural data.
- Divine providence: God's ongoing upholding and governing of all things. Colossians 1:17; Hebrews 1:3. Includes preservation, concurrence, and government.

### 1.3 Anthropology (The Doctrine of Humanity)

**1.3.1 Humanity as Created**
- Created in the image of God (*imago Dei*): Genesis 1:26–27. What constitutes the image — substantive views (reason, will, spirituality), relational views (male–female communion, relationship with God), functional views (dominion, vicegerency), or a combination?
- The constitutional nature of humanity: dichotomy (body and soul/spirit) vs. trichotomy (body, soul, spirit). Reformed consensus leans dichotomist.
- The covenant of works (*foedus operum*): Adam as federal head in a probationary arrangement. Grounded in Genesis 2:16–17; Hosea 6:7 [UNVERIFIED — check translation variants]; Romans 5:12–19.

**1.3.2 The Fall**
- The nature of the first sin: unbelief, pride, and disobedience. Genesis 3:1–7.
- The role of Satan as tempter.
- The consequences of the fall:
  - *Immediate*: guilt, shame, alienation from God, expulsion from Eden. Genesis 3:8–24.
  - *Federal*: Adam's sin is imputed to all his natural descendants. Romans 5:12–19; 1 Corinthians 15:21–22.
  - *Corruption of nature*: every faculty of human nature is marred by sin. Total depravity (see §3.1).
  - *Inability*: the unregenerate person cannot please God or turn to Him apart from grace. Romans 8:7–8.

**1.3.3 Sin**
- The nature of sin: any want of conformity to, or transgression of, the law of God (WSC Q. 14; 1 John 3:4).
- Original sin vs. actual sin.
- The universality of sin: Romans 3:23; 1 Kings 8:46.
- Degrees of sin and the doctrine of total depravity: not that every person is as bad as possible, but that sin corrupts every part of the person.
- The bondage of the will: apart from grace, the will is enslaved to sin. John 8:34; Romans 6:16–20.

### 1.4 Christology (The Doctrine of Christ)

**1.4.1 The Person of Christ**
- *Full Deity*: John 1:1; John 20:28; Colossians 1:15–20; Hebrews 1:1–4. Jesus receives worship, forgives sin, and identifies Himself with YHWH (John 8:58: ἐγώ εἰμι — "before Abraham was, I am").
- *Full Humanity*: John 1:14 ("the Word became flesh"); Philippians 2:5–8; Hebrews 2:14–18. Jesus experiences hunger, thirst, fatigue, sorrow, and death. The necessity of a true human nature for representation and atonement.
- *Sinlessness*: Hebrews 4:15. Could Jesus have sinned? Reformed answer: *non posse peccare* (not able to sin) in light of the hypostatic union.
- *The Hypostatic Union*: Two natures in one person, without confusion, change, division, or separation (Chalcedonian Definition, AD 451).
- *The Virgin Birth*: Matthew 1:18–25; Luke 1:26–38.

**1.4.2 The States of Christ**
- *State of Humiliation*: incarnation, suffering, death, burial, descent (debated). Philippians 2:5–8.
- *State of Exaltation*: resurrection, ascension, session at the right hand of the Father, return in glory. Philippians 2:9–11; Acts 2:32–36; Ephesians 1:20–22.

**1.4.3 The Offices of Christ**
- *Prophet*: Christ reveals God's will for salvation. Deuteronomy 18:15 (the Prophet like Moses, fulfilled in Christ — Acts 3:22); John 1:18.
- *Priest*: Christ offers Himself as a sacrifice and makes continual intercession. Hebrews 7:23–25; Hebrews 9:11–14.
- *King*: Christ rules over all things, particularly His church, and will consummate His kingdom. Psalm 2:6; Matthew 28:18; 1 Corinthians 15:24–28.

**1.4.4 The Atonement**
- *Necessity*: was the atonement strictly necessary, or was it the wisest and most fitting way God chose? Reformed consensus: hypothetically, God could have forgiven without satisfaction, but given His decree, the atonement was necessary.
- *Nature*: Penal substitutionary atonement — Christ bore the penalty of the law in the place of His people. Isaiah 53:4–6 [UNVERIFIED — check hermeneutical application]; Romans 3:25–26; 2 Corinthians 5:21; Galatians 3:13.
- *Extent*: See §3.3 (Limited Atonement / Particular Redemption).
- *Other models*: Christus Victor (triumph over the powers: Colossians 2:15), moral influence, governmental, recapitulation (Irenaeus). Reformed theology affirms penal substitution as central but does not deny complementary motifs.

### 1.5 Pneumatology (The Doctrine of the Holy Spirit)

**1.5.1 The Person of the Holy Spirit**
- The Spirit is a divine person, not an impersonal force. Personal attributes: He speaks (Acts 13:2), teaches (John 14:26), grieves (Ephesians 4:30), intercedes (Romans 8:26), and can be lied to (Acts 5:3–4).
- Divine attributes: eternality (Hebrews 9:14), omnipresence (Psalm 139:7–8), omniscience (1 Corinthians 2:10–11).
- Procession: Reformed theology affirms the *filioque* (the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son). John 15:26.

**1.5.2 The Work of the Holy Spirit**
- *In Creation*: Genesis 1:2 — the Spirit hovers over the waters.
- *In Revelation and Inspiration*: 2 Peter 1:21 — men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Spirit.
- *In the Old Testament economy*: selective, temporary empowering for specific tasks.
- *In the Life of Christ*: conception (Luke 1:35), baptism (Matthew 3:16), empowerment for ministry (Luke 4:14, 18), resurrection (Romans 8:11).
- *In Redemption Applied*:
  - Effectual calling and regeneration (John 3:5–8; §2.2–2.3 below).
  - Indwelling of believers: Romans 8:9, 11; 1 Corinthians 6:19.
  - Sanctification: progressive renewal by the Spirit (see §2.7).
  - Assurance: the Spirit witnesses with our spirit. Romans 8:16.
  - Gifts of the Spirit for the building up of the body. 1 Corinthians 12; Romans 12:4–8.
  - Fruit of the Spirit: Galatians 5:22–23.
- *Continuation vs. Cessationism*: major debate within Reformed circles over whether the "sign gifts" (prophecy, tongues, healing) continue today. Neither position defines Reformed orthodoxy; both sides appeal to Scripture and the Westminster Confession.

### 1.6 Soteriology (The Doctrine of Salvation)

*Note: The Order of Salvation (Ordo Salutis) is treated in detail in Part B (§2). What follows here is a brief locus-level summary to anchor the topic map, with cross-references to the detailed treatment.*

**1.6.1 The Accomplishment and Application of Redemption**
- Distinction between *historia salutis* (redemptive history — what Christ accomplished once for all) and *ordo salutis* (the order in which the benefits of that accomplished redemption are applied to individuals).
- Union with Christ as the central soteriological category: all benefits of salvation flow from being united to Christ by faith through the Spirit. Romans 6:1–11; Ephesians 1:3; 2 Corinthians 5:17.

**1.6.2 The Ordo Salutis (see §2 for full treatment)**
- The logical (not necessarily temporal) order in which God applies salvation. Reformed consensus order: Election → Effectual Calling → Regeneration → Conversion (faith and repentance) → Justification → Adoption → Sanctification → Perseverance → Glorification.

**1.6.3 The Doctrines of Grace (see §3 for full treatment)**
- The five points (TULIP) as the Reformed response to Arminianism, codified at the Synod of Dort (1618–1619): Total Depravity, Unconditional Election, Limited Atonement, Irresistible Grace, Perseverance of the Saints.

### 1.7 Ecclesiology (The Doctrine of the Church)

**1.7.1 The Nature of the Church**
- *The people of God*: 1 Peter 2:9–10.
- *The body of Christ*: 1 Corinthians 12:12–27; Ephesians 1:22–23.
- *The temple of the Holy Spirit*: Ephesians 2:19–22.
- *Visible and invisible church*: the invisible church is the whole number of the elect; the visible church is the gathered community of professing believers and their children.
- *The marks of a true church*: the pure preaching of the Word, the right administration of the sacraments, and church discipline (Reformed *notae ecclesiae*). Belgic Confession, Art. 29.

**1.7.2 Church Government (Polity)**
- *Episcopalian*: rule by bishops in apostolic succession.
- *Presbyterian*: rule by elders (teaching and ruling elders), gathered in graded courts (session, presbytery, general assembly).
- *Congregational*: authority resides in the local congregation; elders and deacons serve under congregational authority.
- *Key NT offices*: elders/overseers (πρεσβύτεροι / ἐπίσκοποι — 1 Timothy 3:1–7; Titus 1:5–9) and deacons (διάκονοι — 1 Timothy 3:8–13).

**1.7.3 The Sacraments**
- *Definition*: visible signs and seals of the covenant of grace, instituted by Christ. The Reformed count two: Baptism and the Lord's Supper.
- *Baptism*: Matthew 28:19. Signifies union with Christ in His death and resurrection (Romans 6:3–4), cleansing from sin, and incorporation into the visible church.
  - Paedobaptism vs. Credobaptism: major intra-Reformed division. Paedobaptists argue from covenant continuity (Colossians 2:11–12 as linking circumcision and baptism); credobaptists argue that baptism always follows personal faith in the NT pattern.
- *The Lord's Supper*: Matthew 26:26–29; 1 Corinthians 11:23–26. Signifies participation in the body and blood of Christ.
  - Views on Christ's presence: Roman Catholic transubstantiation; Lutheran consubstantiation/sacramental union; Reformed spiritual presence (Calvin — the Spirit lifts believers to feed on Christ spiritually, truly, by faith); Zwinglian memorialism.

**1.7.4 The Mission and Marks of the Church**
- Worship, edification, evangelism, and acts of mercy.
- The Great Commission: Matthew 28:18–20.

### 1.8 Eschatology (The Doctrine of Last Things)

**1.8.1 Individual Eschatology**
- *Death*: the separation of body and soul, a consequence of sin. Hebrews 9:27.
- *The Intermediate State*: the conscious existence of the soul between death and resurrection.
  - Believers: with Christ. Philippians 1:23 ("to depart and be with Christ"); 2 Corinthians 5:8 ("away from the body and at home with the Lord").
  - Unbelievers: conscious suffering. Luke 16:23–24.
  - Purgatory: rejected by the Reformed confessions. Christ's atonement is fully sufficient.
- *Immortality of the soul* vs. *resurrection hope*: the biblical emphasis falls on bodily resurrection, not a disembodied eternity.

**1.8.2 The Return of Christ (Parousia)**
- Personal, visible, bodily, and glorious. Acts 1:11; 1 Thessalonians 4:16.
- Imminence and the call to readiness. Matthew 24:42–44.
- Major millennial views:
  - *Amillennialism*: the millennium (Revelation 20:1–6) is the present church age; Christ reigns from heaven with the souls of deceased saints. One general resurrection at Christ's return.
  - *Postmillennialism*: the gospel will progressively triumph, bringing a golden age (millennium) before Christ returns.
  - *Historic Premillennialism*: Christ returns before a literal, earthly 1,000-year reign.
  - *Dispensational Premillennialism*: a pre-tribulation rapture, a seven-year tribulation, Christ's return, and a literal, Jewish-oriented millennium.

**1.8.3 The Resurrection of the Dead**
- Universal resurrection: both the just and the unjust. John 5:28–29; Acts 24:15.
- The resurrection body: imperishable, glorious, powerful, spiritual (i.e., animated by the Spirit). 1 Corinthians 15:35–49.

**1.8.4 The Final Judgment**
- Christ as Judge. Matthew 25:31–46; Acts 10:42; 2 Corinthians 5:10.
- Judgment according to works, not as a basis for justification (which is by faith alone), but as evidence of the genuineness of faith or unbelief. Romans 2:6–11 [UNVERIFIED — check whether this refers to believers or is Paul's generic principle, which must be integrated with justification by faith].
- Degrees of reward and punishment.

**1.8.5 The Eternal State**
- *New Heavens and New Earth*: Revelation 21:1–4. The redeemed dwelling with God.
- *Hell*: eternal, conscious punishment. Matthew 25:46; Revelation 14:11 [UNVERIFIED — check interpretive frameworks]. Debated: annihilationism is a minority position within broader evangelicalism, almost entirely rejected by confessional Reformed theology.
- *The Beatific Vision*: seeing God face to face. 1 Corinthians 13:12; Revelation 22:4.

## 2. PART B — Ordo Salutis (Order of Salvation)

The *ordo salutis* is the logical (not necessarily chronological) sequence in which the Holy Spirit applies the benefits of Christ's accomplished redemption to the elect. It answers the question: "In what order do the saving benefits of Christ come to the individual believer?" The Reformed tradition insists that the ordo is a logical schema for understanding distinct aspects of salvation, not a step-by-step timeline; many of these stages are simultaneous in experience.

Grace precedes and enables every step. The ordo is a chain whose links all hold together: those whom God predestines, He also calls, justifies, sanctifies, and glorifies (Romans 8:29–30 — the *golden chain* of salvation).

### 2.1 Election / Predestination

**Definition:** God's eternal, sovereign, and gracious choice of certain individuals out of the fallen mass of humanity to be the recipients of saving mercy, unto eternal life in Christ.

**Key Passages:** Ephesians 1:4–5 ("He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world"); Romans 8:29–30; Romans 9:11–13 (Jacob and Esau, "not because of works but because of Him who calls"); 2 Thessalonians 2:13.

**Theological Content:**
- Election is *in Christ*: Christ is the sphere and ground of election, both the Elect One and the mediator through whom election is executed.
- Election is *unconditional*: not based on foreseen faith or works (see §3.2).
- Election is the ultimate source of all saving benefits; it does not operate independently of the means (preaching, faith, etc.) but ordains those means as well.
- *Reprobation*: the corollary of election, God's passing by the non-elect, leaving them to the just consequences of their sin. This is the hardest aspect of the doctrine; Reformed theology maintains that God's decree of reprobation is *preterition* (passing over), not an act that creates sin. Romans 9:21–23.

**Relationship to Adjacent Stages:** Election logically precedes all other stages as the eternal decree underlying them. It does not, in itself, change the person's experience; that begins with effectual calling.

### 2.2 Effectual Calling

**Definition:** The sovereign act of God, through the Word and Spirit, whereby He summons the elect inwardly and irresistibly to faith in Christ. Distinguished from the *external* or *general* call, which goes to all who hear the gospel but may be resisted.

**Key Passages:** John 6:44 ("No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him"); Romans 8:30 ("those whom He predestined He also called"); Acts 16:14 (Lydia: "the Lord opened her heart to pay attention to what Paul said").

**Theological Content:**
- Effectual calling is an act of divine power, not merely persuasion. It creates the response it commands.
- It is mediated through the preaching of the gospel (Romans 10:14–17); the external call is the ordinary means by which the internal call is issued.
- It is *irresistible* in the sense that God's grace overcomes the sinner's natural resistance without violating the will (see §3.4).

**Relationship to Adjacent Stages:** Effectual calling is the temporal point at which election breaks into the believer's biography. It produces — simultaneously or nearly so — regeneration. The external call precedes; the effectual call ushers in new life.

### 2.3 Regeneration

**Definition:** The sovereign, monergistic (God-alone) work of the Holy Spirit by which a spiritually dead sinner is given new spiritual life — a new heart, a new disposition, and a new governing principle of faith and obedience.

**Key Passages:** John 3:3–8 ("unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God"); Titus 3:5 ("He saved us... by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit"); Ezekiel 36:26–27 ("I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you"); 2 Corinthians 5:17 ("if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation").

**Theological Content:**
- Regeneration is the implantation of spiritual life into a soul that was formerly dead in trespasses and sins (Ephesians 2:1, 5).
- It is a *change of nature*, not merely a change of behaviour or conviction. It affects the whole person: mind, affections, and will.
- It is *below consciousness*: the person does not perceive the moment of regeneration itself, but perceives its effects — faith, repentance, love for God.
- The Reformed order places regeneration *prior to* faith (not temporally, but logically): regeneration is God's act that enables the sinner to believe; faith is the sinner's first act as one now made alive. The Arminian alternative places faith before regeneration (synergism).

**Relationship to Adjacent Stages:** Regeneration immediately produces conversion (faith and repentance). It is the bridge between effectual calling and conversion; calling addresses the person; regeneration changes the person; conversion is the person's response.

### 2.4 Conversion (Faith and Repentance)

**Definition:** The twofold human response to the gospel, enabled by regeneration: turning from sin (repentance) and turning to Christ in trust (faith). Conversion is a single act with two distinguishable aspects.

**2.4.1 Faith**
- *Definition*: A whole-souled trust in and reliance upon Jesus Christ alone for salvation, involving knowledge (*notitia*), assent (*assensus*), and trust (*fiducia*).
- *Key Passages*: Ephesians 2:8–9 ("saved by grace through faith"); Romans 10:9–10; Hebrews 11:1, 6.
- *Instrumental role*: Faith is the alone instrument of justification; it receives and rests on Christ and His righteousness. It is not the ground of justification (that is Christ's righteousness), but the empty hand that receives it.
- *Gift of God*: Faith is itself a gift, not a meritorious human work. Philippians 1:29 ("it has been granted to you... to believe in Him"); Ephesians 2:8 (the entire salvation complex, including faith, is "not of yourselves; it is the gift of God" — the antecedent of τοῦτο in the Greek is debated, but the point holds for Reformed theology regardless of the grammar).

**2.4.2 Repentance**
- *Definition*: A sincere turning from sin, involving intellectual recognition of sin (*notitia*), emotional sorrow for sin (*contritio* / *dolor*), and a volitional change of direction (*conversio* / change of life). The Westminster Shorter Catechism (Q. 87) defines it as "a saving grace, whereby a sinner, out of a true sense of his sin, and apprehension of the mercy of God in Christ, doth, with grief and hatred of his sin, turn from it unto God, with full purpose of, and endeavour after, new obedience."
- *Key Passages*: Acts 3:19 ("Repent therefore, and turn back"); 2 Corinthians 7:10 ("godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation"); Luke 24:47.
- Faith and repentance are inseparably joined; you cannot turn to Christ without turning from sin, and you cannot truly turn from sin without turning to Christ.

**Relationship to Adjacent Stages:** Conversion is the first conscious human response effected by regeneration. It is the instrument that receives justification. The Reformed order is: regeneration → faith/repentance → justification.

### 2.5 Justification

**Definition:** A definitive, once-for-all forensic (legal) act of God, in which He declares the believing sinner righteous in His sight, not by infusing righteousness into the sinner, but by *imputing* (crediting) the perfect righteousness of Christ to the sinner, received through faith alone.

**Key Passages:** Romans 3:21–26 (the locus classicus: righteousness through faith in Jesus Christ, propitiation by His blood, God just and the justifier); Romans 4:3 (Abraham — "Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness"); Romans 5:1 ("Therefore, having been justified by faith, we have peace with God"); Galatians 2:16 ("a person is not justified by works of the law, but through faith in Jesus Christ"); 2 Corinthians 5:21 ("so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God").

**Theological Content:**
- *Forensic, not transformative*: justification is a declaration, not a process. This is the great Reformation insight contra Rome. The Latin *iustificare* in the Vulgate could mean "make righteous"; Luther and Calvin insisted the Pauline δικαιοῦν means "declare righteous."
- *Double Imputation*: the believer's sin is imputed to Christ, and Christ's righteousness (both active and passive obedience) is imputed to the believer.
- *Active Obedience of Christ*: Christ's lifelong fulfillment of the law, credited to believers. Debate exists within Reformed circles whether this is distinct from or subsumed within the general concept of Christ's righteousness.
- *Sola Fide*: justification is by faith *alone*, apart from works of the law. Faith is the sole instrument of justification, though it is never alone (true faith bears fruit: Galatians 5:6; James 2:17–26).
- *Sola Gratia*: justification is by grace alone; faith is the receiving instrument, not a meritorious cause.

**Relationship to Adjacent Stages:** Justification logically follows faith (the instrument) and precedes sanctification, adoption, perseverance, and glorification. It is a definitive act — once declared, it is never undone. There is no double jeopardy in the divine court.

### 2.6 Adoption

**Definition:** The act of God's free grace by which justified sinners are received into the family of God, given all the rights and privileges of children, and enabled to cry "Abba, Father."

**Key Passages:** Romans 8:15–17 ("you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'"); Galatians 4:4–7 ("God sent forth his Son... to redeem those under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons"); John 1:12 ("to all who received him... He gave the right to become children of God").

**Theological Content:**
- Adoption is a distinct benefit, not merely a synonym for justification or regeneration, though it is closely linked to both. Justification changes our *status* before God as Judge; adoption changes our *relationship* to God as Father.
- It confers: access to the Father in prayer, the Father's fatherly care and discipline (Hebrews 12:5–11), the inheritance of eternal life (Romans 8:17 — "heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ"), and the indwelling Spirit as the seal of our sonship.
- There is a "now, not yet" dimension: we are already children of God (1 John 3:1), but we await the full revelation of our sonship at the resurrection (Romans 8:23 — the redemption of our bodies).

**Relationship to Adjacent Stages:** Adoption is logically subsequent to justification (we must be declared righteous before we can be received as children). It runs concurrently with sanctification during the Christian life and finds its completion in glorification.

### 2.7 Sanctification

**Definition:** The progressive, lifelong work of God and the believer, by which the Holy Spirit renews the believer into the image of Christ, progressively mortifying sin and vivifying new life.

**Key Passages:** Romans 6:1–14 (dead to sin, alive to God in Christ); 2 Corinthians 3:18 ("beholding... we are being transformed from one degree of glory to another"); Philippians 2:12–13 ("work out your own salvation... for it is God who works in you"); 1 Thessalonians 4:3 ("this is the will of God, your sanctification"); Hebrews 12:14 ("strive... for the holiness without which no one will see the Lord"); 1 Peter 1:15–16 ("be holy, for I am holy").

**Theological Content:**
- *Definitive vs. progressive sanctification*: In one sense, believers are already sanctified (set apart for God; positional holiness: 1 Corinthians 1:2). In another sense, sanctification is ongoing (progressive conformity to Christ).
- *The agent*: the Holy Spirit is the primary agent. The believer is simultaneously active (striving, putting to death, pursuing) and passive (being transformed, relying on grace).
- *Mortification and vivification*: the twin aspects — putting sin to death and bringing new obedience to life. Classic treatment: John Owen, *Of the Mortification of Sin in Believers*.
- *The role of the law*: the moral law (summarised in the Ten Commandments) serves as the rule of sanctification, not as a means of justification. The "third use of the law" (*tertius usus legis*): the law as a guide for grateful obedience.
- *Perfectionism*: rejected by Reformed confessions. Sanctification is progressive but never complete in this life. 1 John 1:8: "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves."

**Relationship to Adjacent Stages:** Sanctification logically follows justification and adoption, and it continues throughout the Christian life until death. It is distinct from justification: justification is a once-for-all declaration of righteousness; sanctification is a progressive transformation in righteousness. They are inseparable but distinguishable. Sanctification merges into glorification at death/resurrection.

### 2.8 Perseverance of the Saints

**Definition:** The gracious, sovereign preservation and persistence of all true believers: God ensures that those who are truly regenerated and justified will never totally or finally fall away, but will be kept by God's power through faith unto salvation.

**Key Passages:** John 10:28–29 ("I give them eternal life, and they will never perish... no one is able to snatch them out of the Father's hand"); Romans 8:38–39 ("nothing will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord"); Philippians 1:6 ("He who began a good work in you will bring it to completion"); 1 Peter 1:5 ("who by God's power are being guarded through faith for a salvation ready to be revealed").

**Theological Content:**
- Perseverance is *both* God's preservation of the believer (the divine side) *and* the believer's continuing in faith and holiness (the human side). The former guarantees the latter.
- The means by which God preserves His people include the warning passages of Scripture. The warnings are real; they are one of the instruments God uses to keep His people. Hebrews 6:4–8 and Hebrews 10:26–31 are the classic difficult texts.
- *Temporary faith*: Scripture acknowledges that some profess faith for a time but later fall away (the parable of the sower: Matthew 13:20–21; 1 John 2:19: "they went out from us... that it might become plain that they all are not of us"). This is not the loss of true saving faith but the exposure of spurious faith.
- Assurance is distinct from perseverance: a true believer may lack assurance for a season, but God will preserve them nonetheless. This is debated intra-Reformed: some Puritans made assurance a separate, subsequent gift; others held that assurance belongs to the essence of faith.

**Relationship to Adjacent Stages:** Perseverance bridges sanctification and glorification. It is the guarantee that the one who has been called, regenerated, converted, justified, adopted, and being sanctified *will* arrive at glorification. The golden chain holds: Romans 8:30 — "those whom He justified, He also glorified" (aorist tense, viewing the whole as a completed certainty).

### 2.9 Glorification

**Definition:** The final, completed stage of salvation, in which believers — at Christ's return — are fully and finally conformed to the image of Christ, body and soul, with all sin removed and all the effects of the fall reversed.

**Key Passages:** Romans 8:30 ("those whom He justified, He also glorified"); 1 Corinthians 15:50–55 ("we shall all be changed... the dead will be raised imperishable"); Philippians 3:20–21 ("He will transform our lowly body to be like His glorious body"); 1 John 3:2 ("we shall be like Him, because we shall see Him as He is").

**Theological Content:**
- Glorification includes the resurrection of the body and the full renovation of the believer into the image of Christ. It is the "redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23).
- It removes every trace of sin — no more indwelling sin, no more temptation, no more suffering or sorrow. Revelation 21:4.
- Glorification is the terminus of the ordo salutis and the gateway to the eternal state (see §1.8.5). There is continuity between the person on earth and in glory, but also radical transformation.
- There are degrees of glory: all glorified believers are perfectly blessed, but some enjoy greater capacity for glory. The concept — drawn from passages like 1 Corinthians 15:41–42 and Daniel 12:3 [UNVERIFIED — check exegesis] — is debated but has a pedigree in Reformed dogmatics.

**Relationship to Adjacent Stages:** Glorification is the completion of sanctification and the consummation of all prior stages. It is a simultaneous event for all believers at the *parousia*. After glorification, there is no further stage; the believer enters the eternal state.

## 3. PART C — The Doctrines of Grace (TULIP)

The so-called Five Points of Calvinism are not a summary of Calvin's theology, but the Reformed response to the Five Articles of the Remonstrance (1610), codified at the Synod of Dort (1618–1619). They address the specific points of controversy raised by the followers of Jacobus Arminius. TULIP is a convenient (English-language) mnemonic that did not originate at Dort itself.

**Important qualifier:** TULIP does not exhaust Reformed soteriology. It is a polemical precision instrument developed to answer specific errors, not a positive statement of the whole Reformed faith. Many Reformed theologians accept the substance of the five points while finding TULIP an incomplete or potentially misleading packaging.

### 3.1 Total Depravity (Radical Corruption)

**Definition:** As a consequence of the Fall, every part of human nature — mind, will, affections, and body — is corrupted by sin. The unregenerate person is spiritually dead, unable to please God, and unable to turn to Christ apart from regenerating grace. "Total" means the corruption extends to the *totality* of the person, not that every person is as evil as possible or that humans are incapable of any civil good.

**Key Proof-Texts:**
- Ephesians 2:1–3: "And you were dead in the trespasses and sins..."
- Romans 3:10–12: "None is righteous, no, not one; no one understands; no one seeks for God. All have turned aside..."
- Romans 8:7–8: "For the mind that is set on the flesh is hostile to God... it cannot submit to God's law... Those who are in the flesh cannot please God."
- John 6:44: "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him." The Greek δύναται indicates inability, not mere unwillingness.
- John 8:34: "Everyone who commits sin is a slave to sin."

**Explanation:**
Total depravity does not deny that unregenerate people can perform acts of civic virtue, love their families, or create great art. What it denies is that any such acts can be done with a right motive toward God (faith and love), and it denies that the unregenerate will can move toward God for salvation without prior, enabling grace. The sinner is not merely sick or wounded; he is dead (Ephesians 2:1). The will is in bondage to sin and must be liberated by God before it can choose Christ.

This is the foundation for the remaining four points: because the sinner is spiritually dead, election must be unconditional (§3.2), the atonement must be applied effectually (§3.3, 3.4), and salvation must be preserved by God (§3.5).

### 3.2 Unconditional Election

**Definition:** God's choice of certain individuals to salvation is not based on any foreseen faith, good works, or merit in them, but is grounded solely in His own sovereign good pleasure and eternal purpose. The elect are chosen "in Christ" (Ephesians 1:4) from before the foundation of the world, not because of anything in them or done by them.

**Key Proof-Texts:**
- Ephesians 1:4–5: "He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world... In love He predestined us for adoption as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of His will."
- Romans 9:11–13: "Though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad — in order that God's purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of Him who calls — she was told, 'The older will serve the younger.' As it is written, 'Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.'"
- Romans 8:29–30: The golden chain. Foreknowledge (πρόγνωσις, used here of persons, not events) is the origin of predestination. Reformed exegesis understands this "foreknowledge" as a relational knowing beforehand (cf. Amos 3:2 [UNVERIFIED — check parallel usage]), not a mere prescience of faith.
- 2 Timothy 1:9: "who saved us and called us to a holy calling, not because of our works but because of His own purpose and grace, which He gave us in Christ Jesus before the ages began."
- Acts 13:48: "And as many as were appointed to eternal life believed."

**Explanation:**
Unconditional election is the positive counterpart to total depravity: because the sinner cannot and will not choose God, God must choose the sinner if anyone is to be saved. The Arminian alternative — election based on foreseen faith — makes God's decree depend on the creature's autonomous choice, which Reformed theology sees as inconsistent with the biblical pattern of divine sovereignty and undermining of *sola gratia*.

Election is not arbitrary or capricious; it is grounded in the wisdom and love of God. The *reason* for the election of particular individuals is hidden in God's inscrutable will (Deuteronomy 29:29). The *ground* of election is Christ; the *goal* is holiness and doxology (Ephesians 1:4, 6, 12). Election does not diminish the free offer of the gospel: the gospel call goes to all, and all who come are received. The identity of the elect is known only by their response to the gospel.

### 3.3 Limited Atonement (Particular Redemption)

**Definition:** In the death of Christ, God intended actually and infallibly to secure the salvation of the elect — and of the elect alone. Christ's atoning work was sufficient for all, but efficient only for the elect. He died particularly for His sheep, His church, His people.

**Key Proof-Texts:**
- John 10:11, 15: "The good shepherd lays down His life *for the sheep*." The atonement is specifically for the sheep, not for wolves or goats.
- Ephesians 5:25: "Christ loved the church and gave Himself up *for her*."
- Acts 20:28: "the church of God, which He obtained with His own blood."
- Romans 8:32: "He who did not spare His own Son but gave Him up for us all [the elect; see context from v. 29–30], how will He not also with Him graciously give us all things?"
- Matthew 1:21: "He will save *His people* from their sins."

**Explanation:**
The term "limited atonement" is often misunderstood. Every theological system limits the atonement in some way. Arminianism limits the *efficacy* of the atonement (it does not actually save anyone unless faith is added to it). Reformed theology limits the *design* or *intent* of the atonement (it was intended to save the elect). The question is not *whether* the atonement is limited, but *how*.

If Christ died for all people in exactly the same way, and some are finally lost, then Christ's death does not actually save — it merely makes salvation possible. The Reformed position is that Christ's death actually, infallibly accomplishes the salvation of all for whom He died. He did not die to make salvation possible; He died to save. The atonement is definite, not hypothetical.

The sufficiency/efficiency distinction is crucial: Christ's death is of infinite worth and is objectively sufficient to atone for the sins of the whole world (John 3:16 is a genuine declaration of God's love and a genuine offer). But it is applied efficiently only to those who believe — the elect. John Murray preferred the term "definite atonement."

This is the most contested of the five points, even among those otherwise Reformed. Hypothetical universalists (sometimes called "four-point Calvinists" or Amyraldians, after Moïse Amyraut) hold that Christ died for all in a certain sense, while upholding the other four points. The debate turns on the nature of intent, the interpretation of "world" (κόσμος) passages (e.g., John 3:16; 1 John 2:2), and the logical coherence of universal atonement with unconditional election.

### 3.4 Irresistible Grace (Effectual Grace)

**Definition:** When God calls the elect to salvation through the inward, effectual call of the Spirit, that call unfailingly brings about the sinner's willing response of faith. Grace is irresistible not in the sense that the sinner's will is coerced or violated, but in the sense that God's grace so transforms the will that the sinner freely and joyfully embraces Christ. The regenerating work of the Spirit overcomes all resistance and creates new desires.

**Key Proof-Texts:**
- John 6:37: "All that the Father gives me *will* come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out."
- John 6:44: "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him, and I will raise him up on the last day." The connection between drawing and raising guarantees the outcome.
- Acts 16:14: "The Lord opened [Lydia's] heart to pay attention to what was said by Paul."
- John 3:3–8: The new birth is likened to the wind (πνεῦμα): it blows where it wishes; it is sovereign, not controlled by the one born.

**Explanation:**
The term "irresistible" is liable to misunderstanding. Better alternatives are "effectual grace" or "invincible grace." The point is not that the sinner never resists — all sinners resist the gospel until regenerated. The point is that when God's effectual call comes, all resistance is overcome. God does not drag unwilling sinners kicking and screaming into the Kingdom; He makes unwilling sinners willing. As Augustine put it, God's grace is "indelightful" [UNVERIFIED — check exact Augustinian citation; the concept of *delectatio victrix* ("victorious delight") is well-attested, but verify the precise formulation].

The Arminian alternative posits "prevenient grace" — a grace given to all that enables but does not ensure a free response of faith. The Reformed objection is that this makes the decisive difference between believers and unbelievers not grace, but the human will. If grace enables but does not ensure, and some believe while others do not, then the determining factor is ultimately the human choice, not God's grace — which contradicts *sola gratia* and renders effectual calling ineffectual.

Irresistible grace does not nullify the will; it restores the will. The will is not destroyed but renewed. The liberated will acts according to its new nature and therefore freely chooses Christ. As the Westminster Confession 10.1 states, God "doth persuade and enable us to embrace Jesus Christ, freely offered to us in the gospel."

### 3.5 Perseverance of the Saints

**Definition:** All those whom God has chosen, called, regenerated, and justified will — by the preserving grace of God and the indwelling Spirit — continue in faith and holiness to the end and will be finally saved. They may fall into serious sin and for a season backslide, but they will never totally or finally fall from a state of grace.

**Key Proof-Texts:**
- John 10:28–29: "I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand. My Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all, and no one is able to snatch them out of the Father's hand."
- Romans 8:38–39: "For I am sure that neither death nor life... nor anything else in all creation will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord."
- Philippians 1:6: "And I am sure of this, that he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ."
- 1 Peter 1:5: "who by God's power are being guarded through faith for a salvation ready to be revealed in the last time."
- Romans 8:30: "those whom he justified he also glorified." No justified person fails to reach glorification.

**Explanation:**
See also §2.8 above. This doctrine is the logical consequence of unconditional election, particular redemption, and irresistible grace. If God has chosen a people, sent His Son to die particularly for them, and effectually called them, it is unthinkable that any for whom Christ died would finally be lost. Perseverance is the outworking of the unbreakable unity of the Trinity in salvation.

The doctrine does not teach "once saved, always saved" in a cheap-grace sense that permits licentious living. True perseverance is perseverance *in faith and holiness*. Those who profess faith but later repudiate Christ and live in unrepentant sin demonstrate by their falling away that their profession was never genuine (1 John 2:19). The perseverance of the saints includes the *persistence* of the saints: God's preservation never operates apart from the believer's continuing faith.

The warning passages (e.g., Hebrews 6:4–8; 10:26–31) serve as means God uses to preserve His people: they produce holy fear and earnest vigilance, not carnal presumption.

## 4. PART D — Theme-Frequency Method

This section describes a method for identifying and quantifying the relative prominence of theological themes within a given passage, sermon, chapter, or corpus. It is designed to serve the parser aide's task of mapping a text's doctrinal content against the systematic theology taxonomy established in Parts A–C.

### 4.1 Purpose and Rationale

The theme-frequency method answers the question: *What, doctrinally speaking, is this text "about"? What weight does it give to each locus?* It provides a structured bridge between exegesis (what the text says) and systematics (how the text maps onto doctrinal categories). For a pastor or teacher, it helps ensure that preaching and teaching are not lopsided — that the counsel of God is proclaimed in proportion to Scripture's own emphases.

It is not a substitute for exegesis or theological judgment. It is a heuristic scaffold.

### 4.2 Step 1 — Define the Taxonomy

The method uses the eight loci from Part A (§1.1–§1.8) as its primary categories, with the following refinements for scoring:

| Code | Locus | Sub-Topics (non-exhaustive) |
|------|-------|----------------------------|
| A    | Prolegomena | Revelation, Scripture (inspiration, authority, sufficiency, canon), theological method, hermeneutics |
| B    | Theology Proper | Attributes of God, Trinity, decrees, creation, providence |
| C    | Anthropology | Image of God, fall, sin (original, actual, depravity), human constitution |
| D    | Christology | Person (deity, humanity, hypostatic union), states, offices, atonement |
| E    | Pneumatology | Person of the Spirit, work in redemption, gifts, fruit, indwelling |
| F    | Soteriology | Ordo Salutis stages (election, calling, regeneration, conversion, justification, adoption, sanctification, perseverance, glorification), union with Christ, grace |
| G    | Ecclesiology | Nature of church, marks, government, sacraments, mission |
| H    | Eschatology | Death, intermediate state, parousia, resurrection, judgment, eternal state |

Additionally, two cross-cutting themes are tracked:
- **Gospel/Grace** (G/G): references to the good news, free grace, unmerited favour, the indicative of salvation.
- **Imperative/Duty** (I/D): commands, exhortations, law, ethics, Christian living.

### 4.3 Step 2 — Unitisation

Divide the text into units of analysis. For a sermon transcript or chapter, this could be:

- *Paragraph-level scoring*: each paragraph (or roughly every 3–5 sentences) is scored once, with up to two primary loci assigned.
- *Pericope-level scoring*: for Scripture passages, follow natural narrative or rhetorical units.
- *Verse-level scoring*: for dense theological passages (e.g., Romans 3–8, Ephesians 1), finer granularity may be warranted.

The choice of unitisation depends on the intended precision and the length of the text. For a sermon, paragraph-level is usually sufficient.

### 4.4 Step 3 — Scoring Rules

For each unit, assign up to **two dominant loci codes**, with the following priority rules:

1. **Explicit Content:** Score what the text explicitly states, not what is merely implied. A verse about faith in Christ scores as Soteriology (F), even if a theologian *could* derive an implication about the atonement's extent.

2. **Primary Category Preference:** If a unit could be coded under two categories, prefer the category the passage itself foregrounds. For example, Romans 8:29–30 mentions foreknowledge, predestination, calling, justification, and glorification — score Soteriology (F), because the passage is explicitly about the order of salvation, even though it touches on Theology Proper (God's decree).

3. **One Passage, Two Codes:** Many passages legitimately touch two loci. For example:
   - John 1:1–14 → D (Christology) + B (Theology Proper — the Word was God)
   - Ephesians 4:30–32 → E (Pneumatology — grieve not the Spirit) + I/D (Imperative/Duty — put away bitterness, be kind)
   - Romans 5:12–21 → C (Anthropology — sin entered through one man) + D (Christology — the obedience of the one)

4. **Gospel/Grace vs. Imperative/Duty Tagging:** In addition to the primary locus code, tag each unit with **G** (gospel/grace indicative) or **I** (imperative/duty), based on the dominant mood of the passage. If both are present, score both.

5. **Noise Threshold:** Units that are purely transitional, narrative connective tissue, or administrative (e.g., "And it came to pass...," greetings, travelogues) may be scored as **X** (null/uncategorised) and excluded from the percentage calculation.

### 4.5 Step 4 — Aggregation and Weighting

After scoring all units, compute two metrics:

**4.5.1 Simple Frequency (% of Units)**
```
Locus X frequency = (Number of units coded X) / (Total scorable units) × 100
```

This gives the raw proportion of attention each locus receives. For example, if a sermon of 30 scorable paragraphs scores 12 under Soteriology (F), the F frequency is 40%.

**4.5.2 Weighted Prominence**
Some passages carry more doctrinal "density" or rhetorical weight than others. A simple frequency count may under-represent a passage that is structurally central to the argument. An optional second pass can apply a weight multiplier:

- **Standard weight (×1):** ordinary expository units.
- **Elevated weight (×2):** the text's thesis statement, climax, doxology, or passage that the speaker explicitly flags as the main point.
- **Peak weight (×3):** the passage explicitly and repeatedly cited as the controlling idea of the whole text (rare — typically only one or two units in a longer text).

Weighting introduces subjectivity; it should be applied transparently and documented. The unweighted frequency should always be reported alongside the weighted score.

**4.5.3 Combined Dominance Score**
```
Dominance Score = (Frequency × 0.6) + (Weighted Frequency × 0.4)
```
This blends the objective count with the weighted assessment, giving primacy to frequency while allowing weighted prominence to adjust the score.

### 4.6 Step 5 — Ordo Salutis Sub-Scoring

If Soteriology (F) is the dominant locus, perform a sub-scoring pass using the nine stages in Part B:

| Sub-Code | Stage              |
|----------|--------------------|
| F1       | Election           |
| F2       | Effectual Calling  |
| F3       | Regeneration       |
| F4       | Conversion (Faith/Repentance) |
| F5       | Justification      |
| F6       | Adoption           |
| F7       | Sanctification     |
| F8       | Perseverance       |
| F9       | Glorification      |

Compute sub-scores as percentages of total F-coded units. This reveals whether the text emphasises the *initiation* of salvation (F1–F5) or the *continuation/completion* (F7–F9), or whether it gives disproportionate attention to one stage (e.g., a sermon series heavily weighted toward sanctification with little on justification).

### 4.7 Step 6 — Doctrinal Profile and Visualisation

Produce a **doctrinal profile** — a concise report of the text's theological emphases:

```
Doctrinal Profile: Romans 3:21–26
=================================
Dominant Locus:    Soteriology (F) — 78%
Sub-dominant:      Theology Proper (B) — 15%
Other:             Christology (D) — 7%
G/G vs I/D ratio:  100% Gospel / 0% Imperative
Ordo Salutis:      F5 (Justification) — 90%; F4 (Faith) — 10%
Summary:           This passage is overwhelmingly a justification text
                   with a secondary emphasis on the righteousness of God
                   displayed in the cross.
```

For sermons and longer texts, a radar/spider chart across the eight loci can serve as a visual "doctrinal fingerprint."

### 4.8 Step 7 — Diagnostic Questions

The parser, having generated the doctrinal profile, can use it to ask diagnostic questions:

1. **Breadth:** Does this text/sermon touch the full sweep of redemptive history, or is it narrowly focused on one locus? Neither is inherently right or wrong; the diagnosis simply flags the profile.

2. **Gospel/Imperative Balance:** Is this text overwhelmingly imperatival without gospel grounding? A high I/D ratio without corresponding G/G content may signal moralism. Conversely, a text that is all gospel-indicative may be appropriate for certain passages (e.g., the opening doxologies of Ephesians or 1 Peter).

3. **Lacunae:** Are there major loci that are entirely absent from the corpus under review? For example, a body of sermons that never touches eschatology or ecclesiology may be missing important biblical themes.

4. **Theocentric vs. Anthropocentric:** Does the profile skew heavily toward Anthropology and Soteriology, with minimal Theology Proper? The danger is that God becomes a supporting character in the story of human redemption.

5. **Ordo Salutis Distribution:** Is there a healthy diet across the stages? A steady emphasis on sanctification without proportionate attention to justification and union with Christ risks moralism. An exclusive emphasis on justification without sanctification risks antinomianism.

### 4.9 Methodological Cautions

1. **Reductionism:** Doctrinal scoring is not the same as understanding the text. A text is more than the sum of its doctrinal references. This method is an aide, not a replacement for the hermeneutical task.

2. **Inter-Coder Reliability:** Different readers will score the same passage differently. The taxonomy is broad enough to accommodate reasonable disagreement, but the method benefits from calibration: multiple scorers comparing results and refining their approach.

3. **Presence ≠ Emphasis:** A passing reference to a doctrine should not be weighted identically to a sustained argument for it. The weighted pass (§4.5.2) partially addresses this, but it remains imperfect.

4. **Text Type Sensitivity:** An epistle may map differently than a historical narrative, a psalm, or apocalyptic. This method is most naturally suited to NT epistles and doctrinal passages; it requires adaptation for other genres.

5. **The Danger of Quotas:** The method should not be used to demand that every text or sermon hit every locus — that would violate the integrity of the text. The goal is *awareness* of emphasis, not *enforcement* of balance.
